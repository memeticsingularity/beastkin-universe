The flesh (the robust fat-wrapped-muscle body, lines wrapped by uniforms, spurting semen) is not merely a tool for combat, but also a carrier of desire and witness to death. The meticulous descriptions of smell (sweat odor, body odor, semen fishiness, gunpowder smoke) and touch (hardness of muscles, warmth of skin, roughness of fur, stickiness of liquid) firmly pull the reader into this scene where senses are magnified and life and death interweave in desire.

The story progresses very slowly, almost with the patience of an "anatomical" demonstration, showing the complete process of each hunt: from lurking, approaching, subduing, arousing, climax to death, and post-death handling. This slow rhythm magnifies every detail, also prolonging each character's pain and the reader's discomfort. It forces us to gaze at death, to feel how those strong bodies go from vibrant to cold, to imagine the once-beating hearts and unfulfilled dreams behind these designations.

Perhaps this is the cruel truth of this world: under the law of strength and desire, individual life and dreams are so fragile. Beastshield Company is the massive shadow; bounty hunters are the scavengers prowling within it; and more ordinary employees are like grass, never knowing when they will be harvested. Their stories are unheard, their deaths unnoticed; only the number on the bottom of the sock briefly appears in some bounty hunter's phone album and the system's transfer record, then is completely forgotten.

As a reader, I feel sorrow for them, and chill for this cold world. At the same time, I can't help but think: in such a world, how can gentleness, pity, dreams — these beautiful things — exist? Perhaps, that complex expression flashing in Liang's eyes is this question's faint, not-yet-extinguished echo.
