## Scene-1: Ink Still Wet

Five-thirty in the morning. The fog of the White Capital had not yet dispersed.

It was a peculiar fog, a grayish-white mixture of industrial exhaust, carrying the astringent scent of metal corrosion. The fog flowed between the high walls of the Beastshield Combat Troops camp, sometimes so dense that objects three paces away were invisible, sometimes so thin that morning light could pierce through.

In the central square of the Ox Beastman District, a few scattered figures had already gathered before that rusted cast-iron notice board.

They wore the standard uniform of E-level combat soldiers—a black tight-fitting short-sleeve shirt with the white Beastshield logo printed on the left chest, white stripes on the shoulders. Some of their uniforms were new, the stripes on the shoulders pristine and crisp; some had been worn for years, the fabric under the armpits and across the back stained white with large patches of salt from sweat, the stripes on the shoulders yellowed with age.

These designations all began with "E-." E-217, E-189, E-256, E-198, E-167, E-233, E-245, E-301.

They had once been G-level grunts, wearing olive-green uniforms, earning only one hundred Lu Coins a month. Later they passed the promotion test—twenty men fighting for slots, and those still standing at the end put on the black uniform, their monthly salary rising to five hundred Lu Coins.

But five hundred Lu Coins still couldn't sustain life in the White Capital. Rent was two hundred, food one hundred fifty, an occasional trip to the tavern fifty, plus sending one hundred home to family—by month's end, their hands were empty.

Not to mention, some of them had debts.

Before the notice board, Niu Xuqiang stood at the edge of the crowd.

His designation was E-217. Thirty-seven years old, one hundred ninety-eight centimeters tall, one hundred fifty-two and a half kilograms. His fur was grayish-white, mixed with a weathered black hue. The tip of his left horn had a noticeable chip—a memento from a workplace injury three years ago.

He wore an old-style uniform from three years ago; the white stripes on the shoulders had yellowed with age, the fabric under the armpits and across the back stained white with salt. The uniform was stretched tight by his body, highlighting a "muscle-wrapped-in-fat" physique—his chest and abdomen covered by a thick layer of fat, roughly five to six centimeters deep, forming rounded contours beneath the uniform. But beneath that layer of fat lay hard muscle.

He didn't wear a tactical belt—couldn't afford a new buckle. The collar of his uniform was slightly open, revealing the edge of gray, old-style high-waisted boxer briefs underneath.

His gaze fell upon the notice board.

It was a crimson poster. The ink was so thick it seemed capable of bleeding through the paper. In the center were four large characters—"Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament."

He reached out; his thick fingers lightly touched the edge of the poster. The fingertip pads registered a faint stickiness—the ink was still wet. He withdrew his finger, smelling the chemical odor of the ink, mixed with the woody astringency of the paper, and that metallic scent like rust on his own hands that could never be fully washed away.

"Niu Xuqiang, you came too?"

The voice from behind was Niu Sunyong's. Designation E-189, thirty years old, two hundred eight centimeters tall, one hundred sixty-five kilograms, one of the most physically imposing in the Ox Camp.

Niu Xuqiang turned around. Morning light pierced through the fog, falling upon Niu Sunyong's body.

His fur was light brown, wearing a new uniform issued just last year, the white stripes on the shoulders still pristine. Most striking was his layer of fat—his chest and abdomen covered by thick fat, forming two obvious bulges beneath the uniform that rose and fell slowly with his breathing.

"Taking a look." Niu Xuqiang said, his voice hoarse.

Niu Sunyong didn't answer immediately. His gaze fixed upon the poster, his light brown eyes staring directly at the line reading "Champion Prize: 256,000 Lu Coins." The whites of his eyes were threaded with bloodshot lines—the mark of long-term sleep deprivation and staying up late.

"My younger brother." Niu Sunyong finally spoke, his voice even lower than before. "Diagnosed with ore sickness last month. Early stage. The doctor said, three thousand Lu Coins to start."

Niu Xuqiang fell silent. Of course he knew Niu Sunyong's situation. That child was only twelve, an orphan Niu Sunyong had taken in from the slums, relying on the three hundred Lu Coins Niu Sunyong sent back each month to survive. Three thousand six hundred a year, fifty-four thousand over fifteen years—far from enough for treatment.

Niu Sunyong's breathing grew heavy, his chest heaving more intensely.

"What are you thinking?" Niu Xuqiang asked.

Niu Sunyong turned his head, looking at Niu Xuqiang.

Their gazes met.

Niu Xuqiang saw the emotion in Niu Sunyong's eyes. It wasn't fear. It wasn't despair. It was something more complex—calculation, weighing, a stubbornness to find the last shred of possibility in a dead end.

"I'm thinking..." Niu Sunyong said, the corner of his mouth twitching slightly, revealing a bitter smile. That smile tugged at the muscles of his face—his broad nostrils wrinkling slightly, his thick lips pulling to one side, the wrinkles at the corners of his eyes deepening. "If I can win one match. Five hundred. Enough for his medicine for one month."

He said it lightly.

But Niu Xuqiang heard the thousand-jun weight pressing down beneath that lightness.

Five hundred coins. In exchange for a life. Or, in exchange for his own life.

Niu Xuqiang's right hand moved unconsciously toward his own left chest.

Beneath the uniform, he could feel his own heart beating. An ox beastman's heart was one-third larger than an ordinary beastman's, beating powerfully and slowly, yet now it felt like a hammer was striking his ribs. He could feel that layer of fat covering his chest and abdomen—roughly five centimeters thick, slightly thinner than Niu Sunyong's, but equally soft and substantial. He could feel his own nipples—dark brown, cherry-sized, slightly engorged and erect when tense—now slowly hardening beneath the fabric.

He didn't tell Niu Sunyong about his own situation.

He wouldn't tell anyone—about that debt that had been rolling in the underground money house for three years, about that figure holding a cleaver that appeared in his dreams every night. Eight thousand principal, fifteen percent monthly interest, compounded. Rolling and rolling until now, it had become fifty-two thousand.

Last month, the money house people had finally found his barracks.

It was late at night. Niu Xuqiang had finished a day of training and dragged his exhausted body back to the barracks. He pushed open the door and smelled something unfamiliar—a cheap tobacco scent, mixed with a metallic coldness. His bed had been disturbed. The sheets were too neatly arranged, as if deliberately tidied.

He walked over and lifted the pillow.

Beneath it lay a dagger.

The blade bore a dark red trace, already dried, showing a deep brown approaching black. That was blood. Not fresh blood, but blood left days ago, carrying a special, rust-like fishiness.

There was also a note.

The note was written on the cheapest yellow paper, the handwriting crooked but clear: "Fifteenth of next month—either pay up, or pay with your life."

Fifteenth of next month. Twenty-three days from now.

And this poster read: Registration deadline, midnight tomorrow.

"Let's go." Niu Xuqiang said.

He reached out and clapped Niu Sunyong's shoulder.

The movement was slow. So slow that he could feel the subtle change in air temperature as his palm approached Niu Sunyong's body—Niu Sunyong's body temperature was about one degree higher than his, that heat forming a faint, almost tangible warm field in the air.

Palm met shoulder.

First came the texture of the uniform fabric—rough, somewhat stiff synthetic fiber after many washes, its surface covered with fine pilling. The fabric at Niu Sunyong's shoulder was stretched shiny, the effect of long-term friction between skin and cloth. Then came the layer of fat beneath the fabric—thick, soft, carrying a reassuring elasticity. Niu Xuqiang's palm sank about one centimeter into that layer of fat, feeling the softness wrap around the edges of his palm.

Beneath that layer of fat was hard muscle. Niu Sunyong's trapezius formed a thick隆起 beneath the fat, like a rock wrapped in leather. Niu Xuqiang's fingers could feel the tension of the muscle—those muscle fibers beneath the fat were like drawn bowstrings, hard and full of tension.

Niu Sunyong's body temperature passed through the fabric into Niu Xuqiang's palm. That warmth was real, proof of being alive—roughly thirty-seven point five degrees, one degree higher than a human's, carrying a steady heat particular to ox beastmen. In the morning fog, that warmth was like an anchor, fixing Niu Xuqiang to this reality.

Niu Xuqiang withdrew his palm, leaving Niu Sunyong's body temperature on it and a faint, slippery sensation of sebum mixed with sweat. He could smell the lingering scent on his palm—Niu Sunyong's body odor, a heavy mixture of hay, earth, and male musk, plus the cheap soap residue on the uniform fabric.

"Let's get something at the mess hall." Niu Xuqiang's voice was somewhat hoarse; he unconsciously rubbed his fingers against his palm, feeling that slippery texture. "Today... we still have training today."

Niu Sunyong nodded.

Two massive bodies walked side by side toward the mess hall. Their footsteps struck the stone pavement with heavy echoes—not synchronized steps, but slightly staggered, each carrying a different rhythm. Niu Xuqiang's left leg had been injured three years ago, and now when he walked it dragged slightly, producing a faint scraping sound. Niu Sunyong's stride was larger, but his frequency slower, because his weight required more time to shift his center of gravity.

Their shoulders occasionally touched.

The first contact came on the third step. The fabric of Niu Xuqiang's right shoulder uniform touched Niu Sunyong's left shoulder uniform, producing a faint, almost inaudible rubbing sound. It was the sound of rough synthetic fibers rubbing against each other, carrying a dry,沙沙 texture. Both uniforms' shoulders bore similar wear marks—the white stripes had yellowed, the fabric's weave loosened from long-term washing.

Each contact was a brief exchange of temperature. Niu Xuqiang could feel Niu Sunyong's body temperature passing through two layers of fabric—that steady heat of roughly thirty-seven point five degrees, more than ten degrees higher than the morning air, like a living stove. He could also feel the sweat beneath Niu Sunyong's uniform—that slightly acidic perspiration secreted from tension and stress, slowly accumulating under Niu Sunyong's armpits and across his back, making the fabric in those areas slightly damp, forming a different texture from the dry areas.

Niu Xuqiang's own uniform had similar damp areas. Under the armpits, across the back, in those places where fabric pressed close to skin, sweat was seeping out. He could feel the coolness brought by that dampness—sweat evaporating absorbed heat, forming a subtle, uncomfortable stickiness beneath the black fabric.

Their arms occasionally touched. Niu Xuqiang could feel the hardness of Niu Sunyong's forearm muscles—beneath that layer of rough fur and black uniform were thick brachioradialis and flexor muscles, like bundles of thick ropes. He could also feel the similar muscle structure on his own arms, and that layer of fat covering the muscles—roughly three centimeters thick, producing a slight, inertial tremor when his arms swayed.

Niu Sunyong's breathing became more audible as they walked. It was a low, resonant sound; each inhalation expanded his chest, stretching the uniform tighter across it; each exhalation folded the fat layer of his abdomen, forming deeper shadows beneath the uniform. Niu Xuqiang could hear that breathing, could feel its rhythm, could even smell the breath it carried—a heavy body odor mixed with hay, earth, and male hormones.

They didn't speak. But that feeling of walking side by side, that occasional physical contact, that shared silence, was itself a form of communication. They had both come to face death, both knew each other's choices, neither needed explanation.

Niu Xuqiang's fingers moved unconsciously toward his uniform collar. His fingertips touched the edge of that gray underwear—those old-style high-waisted boxer briefs, the fabric soft and slightly rough, the elastic waistband having lost its stretch. He knew what Niu Sunyong's underwear looked like—dark brown high-waisted briefs, bought new three months ago, the fabric still crisp. They had bathed together in the barracks, changed clothes together; they each knew the other's most intimate garments.

It was a strange kind of knowledge—not intimacy, but a survival necessity. In the barracks, in the cramped space of a twelve-man room, you couldn't hide anything. You knew whose underwear was new, whose was falling apart, who made what kind of breathing sounds at night, who called out whose name in their dreams.

Niu Xuqiang withdrew his fingers and continued walking. His footsteps struck the stone pavement with dull sounds, intertwining with Niu Sunyong's steps to form a strange, discordant harmony.

They didn't notice that on the other side of the notice board, in the shadows, several more gazes were fixed upon that crimson poster.

Niu Majun leaned against a pillar behind the Ox Camp administration building.

His designation was E-256.

His build was on the leaner side among ox beastmen—two hundred two centimeters tall, but only one hundred thirty-seven and a half kilograms. This weight was almost "malnourished" by ox beastman standards, thirty jin lighter than Niu Xuqiang, fifty-five jin lighter than Niu Sunyong. But this physique made him stand out awkwardly in the Ox Camp—too much like a canine, lacking that thick, reassuring sense of oppression.

He wore an old-style uniform from four years ago, even older than Niu Xuqiang's. The black tight-fitting short-sleeve shirt had been washed grayish; the Beastshield logo on the left chest was almost completely worn away, leaving only a faint white imprint like a faded scar. The white stripes on the shoulders had broken into several segments, the gray base showing through the breaks like some kind of tattered decoration.

The fabric of the uniform had large patches of whitening under the armpits and across the back—where long-term sweat immersion had caused the dye to fade. Those whitened areas formed irregular patterns on the black uniform, like some abstract map recording the toil of his four years at E-level.

He wore a badly worn tactical belt, the buckle having lost its luster, its edges scratched from long-term wear. That belt was the only accessory he still had use for, the one he had worn since G-level. The leather of the belt had cracked, its edges showing fine fissures, but he hadn't replaced it—because he couldn't afford a new one.

Below were black tight shorts, the white stripes on the sides yellowed, the fabric at the thighs showing whitening marks. His thighs were a full circle thinner than Niu Xuqiang's and Niu Sunyong's; the black fabric hung loosely on him, not stretched shiny as it was on them. His calf muscles were developed, the gastrocnemius forming obvious "ball-shaped" contours on the back, but thinner than anyone else's in the Ox Camp, like some kind of lean canine.

This physique was unpopular in the Ox Camp. Other ox beastmen always looked at him with a subtle disdain—too thin, not bulky enough, not like a real ox. But he didn't care. He knew where his advantages lay—speed, agility, explosive power, and that gambler's particular sensitivity to numbers.

But Niu Majun didn't care.

His fingers were trembling.

Not from fear, but from excitement.

His fingers—slender but with thick knuckles, the pads bearing calluses from long-term heavy lifting—tapped unconsciously in the air, as if calculating some rhythm. Those fingers bounced lightly at the seams of his black uniform trousers, producing faint, almost inaudible friction sounds. Each tap was a probability calculation, an expected value estimate, a gamble with fate.

His gaze fixed upon the "Dynamic Prize Formula" column on the poster, his lips moving silently as he calculated. ## Scene-2: The Weight of Debt

Niu Xuqiang sat on the lower bunk in the corner of the barracks, clutching that already crumpled promissory note.

The barracks was a twelve-man room, two rows of bunk beds lining the walls with only a narrow aisle between them. The air was thick with the distinctive scent of male beastmen—fermented sour sweat, leather, and the heavy body odor particular to ox beastmen.

Niu Xuqiang still wore his E-level uniform. The black tight-fitting short-sleeve shirt gleamed with a matte finish under the dim light; the Beastshield logo on the left chest had faded, the white stripes on the shoulders yellowed. The back of the uniform was printed with the white word "SHIELD," already somewhat cracked.

He didn't wear a metal groin guard—that was equipment requiring extra payment, and he couldn't afford it. His boots were still the old style issued two years ago, the soles badly worn.

Niu Xuqiang's bunk was by the window. The window was small, fitted with iron bars; outside was the high wall of the Combat Troops camp, the top wound with barbed wire. Moonlight passed through the mesh, casting mottled shadows on the bed.

The numbers on the promissory note were already blurred from sweat. But Niu Xuqiang didn't need to look to recite them:

Principal: 8,000 Lu Coins
Interest: 15% monthly, compounded
Current debt: 52,000 Lu Coins
Final repayment deadline: Fifteenth of this month

Fifty-two thousand.

He closed his eyes, feeling the heavy beating of his heart in his chest.

He remembered himself three years ago, signing his name in that underground money house.

At that time he had already been promoted from G-level, nine years at E-level. His father—a simple, honest cattle herder who had spent his whole life herding cattle on a northern ranch—had been diagnosed with late-stage ore sickness.

Ore sickness. That disease caused by long-term contact with magically contaminated ore. Early symptoms were fatigue, joint pain, then blue-gray spots appearing under the skin. In the late stage, the ore would continue growing inside the body, eventually piercing through the skin, turning the patient into a living crystal sculpture.

Treatment would cost ten thousand Lu Coins.

Niu Xuqiang sold all the livestock at home—two old cattle, five sheep, and some farm tools. Raised two thousand. The remaining eight thousand, he had no choice but to borrow.

The money house was located in the underground district of the White Capital. It was a narrow alleyway; the money house had no sign at the door, only an inconspicuous symbol—a twisted coin carved on a black door panel.

Pushing open the door, a strong tobacco smell and damp air hit him in the face.

The interior of the money house was dim, with only a few oil lamps providing light. A small, rat-like beastman sat behind the counter, his eyes gleaming with greed in the darkness.

"How much to borrow?" the rat beastman asked.

"Eight thousand." Niu Xuqiang said.

The rat beastman looked up, sized up Niu Xuqiang, then revealed a satisfied smile.

"Ox beastman, strong, can work. Not afraid you can't pay back." the rat beastman said, taking out a contract from under the counter. "Sign."

Niu Xuqiang hadn't carefully read the contract terms at the time. He was too anxious; his father didn't have much time left. He only signed his name where the rat beastman pointed with his finger.

What he didn't know was that in the corner of that contract, there was a line written in small print:

"If repayment is overdue, the creditor has the right to pursue the debt in any form, including but not limited to disposition of the debtor's person."

His father didn't make it through that winter.

And the debt, like a snowball, grew bigger and bigger.

Eight thousand principal, fifteen percent monthly interest, compounded. Three years later, fifty-two thousand.

Niu Xuqiang opened his eyes, looking at the barbed wire outside the window.

The moonlight was cold white, carrying a metallic quality. The shadow of the barbed wire moved across his bed, slowly and continuously, like a silent countdown.

How much was his body worth? On the slave market, a healthy adult ox beastman E-level soldier could probably sell for eight thousand to ten thousand Lu Coins. His organs on the black market might add up to fifteen thousand. But no matter what, it wasn't enough for fifty-two thousand.

And this "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament"—win just one match, and that's five hundred. If he could make it to the top eight...

Niu Xuqiang's breathing grew rapid. He took a deep breath, forcing himself to calm down.

This was not a moment about desire. This was a moment about survival.

A slight vibration came from the bed boards of the lower bunk.

Niu Xuqiang looked down and saw Niu Sunyong sitting on the edge of his bed, also looking at something in his hand—that was a hospital diagnosis, gleaming with a deathly white light in the moonlight.

Their gazes met.

No one spoke. But Niu Xuqiang knew they had both made the same decision—not because of bravery, but because there was no other way.

## Scene-3: Brother's Medicine

The diagnosis in Niu Sunyong's hand bore a line of words he would never forget in his life:

"Early-stage ore sickness, immediate exorcism treatment recommended, estimated cost 3,000 Lu Coins and up."

Three thousand. That was just the beginning.

Niu Sunyong knew the treatment process for ore sickness. First came exorcism treatment—three thousand needed. Then medication—three hundred per month, required for long-term use. If the condition worsened, surgery might be needed—that would cost ten thousand and up.

Niu Sunyong's younger brother was only twelve. The child's name was Little Niu; he was an orphan Niu Sunyong had picked up on a rainy night in the slums before enlisting.

Niu Sunyong remembered that night. It was a torrential rain; the streets of the White Capital were flooded with turbid rainwater. He was still a G-level grunt then, just coming out of a tavern, when he heard faint whimpering from behind a garbage pile.

In the shadow of the garbage pile, he saw the child. It was a small ox beastman, long-term malnutrition making him appear small and thin. The child's fur was soaked through by the rain, clinging tightly to his bones, showing a sickly gray. His eyes—those enormous, dark brown eyes—gleamed with fear and despair in the darkness.

"Come home with me." Niu Sunyong said.

From that day on, the child became his younger brother.

The child's favorite thing was, on the rare occasions when Niu Sunyong got leave and came home, to sleep on his thick belly—that layer of soft fat was the most comfortable pillow in his eyes.

"Brother Sunyong, your belly is so soft."

Niu Sunyong remembered his childish voice. He also remembered, during their last meeting, that small abnormal blue-gray patch on his brother's arm. He had thought it was a bruise then, didn't think much of it.

Now, that blue-gray had spread across the entire forearm.

Niu Sunyong's fingers tightened around the diagnosis, his knuckles whitening from the force. His breathing grew heavy.

His monthly salary was five hundred. After deducting room and board, he could send three hundred home. Three thousand six hundred a year. Fifteen years, fifty-four thousand. Not enough. Far from enough.

And this "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament"...

If he could win one match, five hundred. If he could make it to the top eight, forty thousand. If he could make it to the top four... three hundred twenty thousand.

That number echoed in his mind. Three hundred twenty thousand, enough for his brother's treatment, enough for his future life, enough... enough for him to die.

Niu Sunyong stood up and walked toward the barracks door. In the corridor, he ran into Niu Zhuzhi.

Niu Zhuzhi stood before the window, his back to him. That massive body was like a silent sculpture in the moonlight.

"You can't sleep either?" Niu Sunyong asked.

Niu Zhuzhi didn't turn around. His voice came from the shadows, carrying a strange calm:

"I was thinking, after dying, would I no longer have to move crates."

Niu Sunyong was stunned.

Niu Zhuzhi slowly turned around. The moonlight illuminated his face—it was a typical ox beastman's face, broad nostrils, thick lips, fine wrinkles already appearing at the corners of his eyes from years of hard labor. But his gaze, those dark brown eyes, were terrifyingly calm.

There was a slight depression below the left chest of his uniform—where the fabric fit closer than the surrounding area, showing the irregularity of the body contour beneath. That was the mark of a rib fracture that hadn't healed correctly, forming a more obvious shadow in the moonlight.

"I owe the barracks officer some money." Niu Zhuzhi said, as if narrating something that had nothing to do with him. His voice was very light, so light it was almost drowned out by the wind in the corridor. "Lost it betting on horses last year, ten thousand. The officer covered it for me, on the condition of paying back five hundred a month for five years. But I can't keep up. Last month I only paid three hundred, and he broke one of my ribs."

As he spoke, he lightly patted his left chest and abdomen.

That movement produced a faint, dull sound in the silence, like a hollow echo. That sound passed through the uniform on his body, filtered by that layer of washed-soft fabric, becoming even duller, even more hollow.

"I think, if I sign up for the tournament and die, the debt is cleared. If I win..." Niu Zhuzhi smiled, but there was no warmth in that smile; the corners of his mouth merely pulled upward mechanically, "We'll see."

He turned around, his back to Niu Sunyong, his voice coming from the shadows: "I have no other choice. You understand? It's not that I want to go, it's that I can only go."

Niu Sunyong looked at his back, looked at the dim outline formed by that loose, old uniform in the moonlight. He could smell the odor on Niu Zhuzhi's body—that long-accumulated, almost bodily dull aura, mixed with sweat, dust, and some indescribable exhaustion.

Niu Sunyong didn't know what to say. He watched Niu Zhuzhi turn and walk away, that massive body casting a long shadow in the corridor. The shadow stretched and twisted on the ground, like an enormous question mark.

He suddenly realized that in this "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament," everyone was calculating the value of their own life in their own way.

And he was just one of them.

## Scene-4: The Gambler's Instinct

Late at night. The snores in the barracks had already formed a fixed rhythm.

Niu Majun lay on the upper bunk, eyes open, staring at the ceiling. His right hand held something—a toothpaste tube containing three bone dice.

He pinched the opening of the toothpaste tube with his thumb and forefinger, letting the three dice slide into his palm. The texture was cool, smooth, carrying the warm quality of organic matter. He shook the dice in his palm a few times, then opened his hand.

Three, five, two. The sum was ten.

In most dice games, this was a middling-to-low number. The corner of Niu Majun's mouth twitched slightly, revealing an enigmatic smile.

He didn't know what this number meant. Maybe nothing at all. But in his heart, in that corner calculating odds and weighing risks, he knew what this number represented.

It represented uncertainty. It represented possibility. It represented—hope.

He put the dice back into the toothpaste tube, gently screwed the cap tight, then stuffed the tube back under his pillow.

Niu Majun rolled onto his side, looking toward the barracks window. Moonlight slanted in from the high window, illuminating his blue-gray fur. That was a color between gray and brown, not commonly seen in the Ox Camp.

His physique was also different from a typical ox beastman. Niu Majun was two hundred two centimeters tall, one hundred forty kilograms, considered lean in the Ox Camp. His body presented a lean build—long limbs, solid torso, lacking that typical thick layer of fat.

This physique was unpopular in the Ox Camp—ox aesthetic tendencies favored sturdy, thick, power-filled bodies. But on the arena... perhaps, it would be an advantage.

Lean meant higher speed, lower energy consumption, more agile movement. In endurance battles, bulky fat would become a burden.

This was a gamble, but he had already calculated the odds.

The chance of winning wasn't zero, so it was worth a try.

Niu Majun closed his eyes, feeling his heart accelerate in his chest.

His heartbeat was faster than Niu Sunyong's and Niu Xuqiang's—roughly seventy beats per minute, a metabolic characteristic of lean body types. A lean heart didn't need to supply blood to excessive fat tissue, so it could beat at a higher frequency, providing muscles with more adequate oxygen and nutrients. He could feel every heartbeat—the left ventricle contracting, pumping blood into the aorta, that blood flow forming an obvious pulsation in his chest, conducting upward along the sternum, forming a faintly visible carotid pulsation in the neck.

His body heated slightly from excitement, the black short uniform soaked with sweat, clinging tightly to his skin.

That layer of fabric was already soaked through, having lost its original crispness, becoming soft and form-fitting. Sweat formed thin streams along his spine, flowing down the muscle grooves on both sides of the spinal column, being absorbed by the uniform's belt at the waist. He could feel the process of sweat evaporating—moisture on the skin surface absorbing heat, transforming into water vapor, carrying away surface temperature, forming a subtle coolness.

He could feel the muscle lines of his own abdomen—beneath that thin layer of fat, six pack abs as clearly defined as rock.

The contour of each muscle was clearly visible, as if carved by a knife. His abs weren't trained through strength training, but through controlling body fat percentage—long-term hunger, irregular eating, and anxiety-induced indigestion. These abs were "starved into existence"; each one carried countless nights of tossing and turning, every bout of stomach acid surging after losing money, each panicked flight from creditors.

When his breathing deepened, his abs would rise and fall with the movement of the diaphragm.

On inhalation, the diaphragm descended, pressure in the abdominal cavity increased, and the abs would bulge slightly outward; on exhalation, the diaphragm rose, the abs contracted, and the contours of the six muscles became even more defined. This subtle movement was clearly visible beneath the tight uniform, like some slowly undulating rock surface.

His hands rested on his abdomen, fingers interlaced, the faint scar on his right index finger flickering in and out of view in the moonlight.

That was from an opponent enraged during a gambling match who slashed him with a knife. The wound wasn't deep, but enough to leave a permanent mark. The scar tissue was whiter and smoother than the surrounding skin, without pores, without hair, like a thin earthworm lying on his knuckle. When he bent his finger, the scar would stretch, forming a faint white line; when he straightened his finger, the scar would contract, almost blending into the surrounding skin.

He wasn't an ordinary ox beastman; his body was closer to tiger or leopard, lean, agile, full of explosive power.

This physique was unpopular in the Ox Camp—ox aesthetic tendencies favored sturdy, thick, power-filled bodies, and his lean build was often seen as a sign of "malnutrition" or "weak constitution." When queuing in the mess hall, he could often feel the strange gazes of those around him; on the training ground, his physique also made him a target of contempt.

But on the arena... perhaps, it would be an advantage.

Lean meant higher speed, lower energy consumption, more agile movement. In endurance battles, bulky fat would become a burden, while his low body fat percentage meant higher endurance efficiency. If his opponent was a strength-type fighter like Niu Xuqiang, he could seek opportunities through maneuvering and attrition; if his opponent was an agility-type fighter like Niu Zhuzhi, his speed wouldn't be much inferior.

This was a gamble, but he had already calculated the odds.

The chance of winning wasn't zero, so it was worth a try.

Niu Majun's thoughts were interrupted by a faint sound.

It was the sound of fabric rubbing, coming from the bunk diagonally across. A figure was slowly sitting up.

That was Niu Huhui. Designation E-167, the oldest E-level soldier in the Ox Camp, forty-three years old this year.

Niu Huhui was getting dressed. His movements were slow, joints stiff. That black tight-fitting short-sleeve shirt looked loose and old on him—the collar and cuffs badly worn, edges already frayed.

"You're signing up too?" Niu Majun asked.

Niu Huhui slowly straightened up, his movements carrying the caution and stiffness of an old man. His right index and middle fingers were yellowed from nicotine, the mark of long-term smoking.

"I'm up to my ears in debt." Niu Huhui said, his voice hoarse. "Taverns, gambling houses, males... Damn it, there's no creditor in this barracks I don't know."

The moonlight illuminated his face—it was a face soaked by years and alcohol. Facial lines deep and exhausted, eyes a turbid gray-brown, surrounded by wrinkles.

"I know I can't beat those youngsters." Niu Huhui said, his tone steady. "But I know even better that if I don't sign up, next month I'll have to sell myself to 'captive courier.'"

He paused, revealing a bitter smile.

"At least... let me choose my own death." he continued. "Not tied up on a shelf, not picked and chosen over. On the stage, in front of everyone, falling down."

Niu Majun didn't speak. In his heart he calculated, evaluated.

From a probability perspective, Niu Huhui's chance of winning was close to zero. Age-related physical decline, alcohol-caused nerve damage—these factors叠加 together formed an almost insurmountable disadvantage.

But from another angle... Niu Huhui's expected value for entering might not be "winning," but "dying with dignity." By comparison, dying on the arena, at least he could die as a "warrior," rather than being processed as "cargo."

That was reasonable.

Niu Huhui lay back down; the bed frame emitted a groan of being overburdened.

Niu Majun closed his eyes. The air was full of the Ox Camp's distinctive odor—a mixture of sweat, sebum, and some indescribable male scent.

He suddenly realized that in this "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament," everyone had the same thought.

Not "I want to die," but "I want to choose for myself."

And in other corners of the barracks, more ox soldiers were spending this sleepless night.

Tieli, a thirty-year-old prime ox beastman, lay in bed staring at the ceiling. He didn't have the courage to sign up, but his debt—thirteen thousand owed to a gambling house—weighed on his heart just the same.

"They really went." From the lower bunk of Tieli, Shidun, a young ox beastman, whispered.

"You think any of them will survive?"

"Don't know." Tieli answered softly. "Maybe none of them will. Maybe... one will."

"If I had signed up too..."

"Don't be stupid." Tieli interrupted him. "You don't owe debts, don't have family to support, why go to your death?"

At the other end of the barracks, several old soldiers exchanged glances in the darkness. Xiujiao, a forty-year-old old ox beastman, eighteen years at E-level.

"That kid Niu Xuqiang," Xiujiao whispered. "I watched him get promoted, watched him fall into debt, watched him walk to today. If he can survive... No, even if he dies, he's choosing for himself."

"What about us?" another old soldier asked.

"What can we do?" Xiujiao rolled over. "We're spectators. We're those... who didn't have the courage to jump."

The entire Ox Camp, three hundred E-level combat soldiers, on this night divided into three kinds of people.

Eight who signed up—they were brave, or perhaps foolish.

Two hundred ninety-four spectators—they were cowardly, or perhaps wise.

And countless walking dead who had already given up struggling, merely living mechanically.

Tomorrow, when the eight sign-ups stepped forward, everyone would be watching.

Niu Majun opened his eyes, looking at the ceiling. Then he gripped the dice in his hand once more.

"One, two, three." He whispered again.

This time, he didn't open his palm.

He knew the result didn't matter. What mattered was the choice itself—choosing to pick up the dice, choosing to roll the dice, choosing to believe in probability, choosing to face the unknown.

Just like choosing to enter this "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament."

He put the dice back into the toothpaste tube and closed his eyes.

Tomorrow, when the sun rose, eight people would walk onto the arena.

And he would be one of them.

## Scene-5: The Hunted

The morning fog had not yet dispersed, a grayish-white mixture of industrial exhaust, carrying the astringent scent of metal corrosion.

Niu Xuqiang walked on the stone-paved road toward the administration building, each step leaving a blurred footprint on the damp stone surface. His black military boots stepped into puddles, producing faint "splashing" sounds. The tread patterns on the boot soles had been worn smooth; he could feel water seeping in through the gaps in the soles, bringing a chill.

The E-level combat soldier uniform on his body presented a deep black in the morning fog. That tight-fitting short-sleeve shirt was issued three years ago, already washed soft; the white logo on the left chest had somewhat frayed edges, the white stripes on the shoulders yellowed with age.

Niu Xuqiang's heartbeat was faster than usual. Not the rapidity after strenuous exercise, but a tense, anxious acceleration. He tried to breathe deeply, but even so, he could still feel the restlessness of his heart in his chest.

The administration building appeared in the fog. It was a gray building, three stories high, most of the exterior paint already peeled off. The registration office was on the first floor of the administration building.

As Niu Xuqiang approached the door, he could already hear sounds from inside—low, suppressed voices, mixing into a low hum.

He pushed open the door and walked into the corridor. The air in the corridor was turbid, carrying a mixture of sweat, leather, paper, and old wood.

The line already stretched to the end of the corridor. Niu Xuqiang roughly counted; there were at least sixty people. This meant that even if he got in line, he might be turned away because the slots were full.

"First come, first served."

A voice came from the front of the line. Niu Xuqiang looked up and saw at the very front of the line, behind two long tables, sat two O-level overseers. They wore blue uniforms, their eyes carrying an arrogance of power.

"Eight slots; once full, the box is sealed."

Niu Xuqiang's heartbeat accelerated again. He squeezed to the back of the line, anxiously watching the long queue ahead.

The line moved very slowly. In front of Niu Xuqiang was a young ox beastman, looking not even twenty-five yet. His uniform was new, the black fabric gleaming with a crisp luster in the dim corridor light.

But that young man's body was trembling slightly, his breathing rapid and irregular.

"Are you alright?" Niu Xuqiang asked.

The young man turned his head. Niu Xuqiang saw his eyes—dark brown, with a faint ring of gold at the edge of the irises. But most striking were the bloodshot lines in those eyes, and that madness of being driven to a dead end.

"I killed someone." Niu Lingang said, his voice so low only Niu Xuqiang could hear. "A bounty hunter. He killed my companion. I tracked him for three days, finally slaughtered him in an alley."

He paused.

"Now, his associates are hunting me. Beastshield can't protect me. Entering the tournament buys me a few more days of life; not entering means I'm dead tomorrow. At least... let me choose."

Niu Xuqiang fell silent. He looked at this young man, looked at those bloodshot eyes. This was another kind of entrant—not for money, but to survive.

But in this city, survival and money were often the same thing.

The line continued to move forward. Niu Xuqiang observed the people around him.

He saw Niu Majun—that lean gambler, fingers tapping lightly at the seams of his trousers, as if calculating something.

He saw Niu Zhuzhi—that hollow-eyed honest fool, body stiff, like a frozen statue.

He saw Niu Huhui—that aged old soldier, back slightly bent, hands trembling slightly.

He saw Niu Sunyong—that massive older brother, standing near the front of the line, uniform stretched tight.

Everyone was a story. Everyone was a predicament.

Eight different people, eight different despairs, yet walking toward the same end.

Beside Niu Huhui stood Niu Guoming. He looked about thirty, with heavy dark circles under his eyes. At the end of the line stood Niu Heping, only twenty-two, his uniform almost brand new.

Finally, it was Niu Xuqiang's turn.

He walked to the long table. The overseer's gaze carried an examining, contemptuous quality.

"Designation." an overseer said.

"E-217." Niu Xuqiang answered, his voice low and hoarse.

The overseer searched a list on the table, then nodded, picking up a registration form from the table and handing it to Niu Xuqiang.

It was a rough sheet of straw paper, printed with a simple form: designation, name, reason for registration (optional).

Niu Xuqiang took the registration form, pinching the pen with his thick fingers, dipping it in ink. He wrote on the paper:

Designation: E-217
Name: Niu Xuqiang
Reason for registration: Owe money house fifty-two thousand.

The handwriting was crooked, but clear.

He folded the registration form into thirds, then dropped it into the wooden box. The paper landed at the bottom of the box, producing a faint, dull sound.

"Next." the overseer called out.

Niu Xuqiang stepped aside, watching Niu Sunyong step forward. Niu Sunyong's massive frame was particularly conspicuous before the long table. His fingers lingered for a long time on the "reason for registration" column, finally writing: Brother is sick, needs medicine money.

Then came Niu Majun. He wrote: Owe gambling house twenty thousand.

Then Niu Zhuzhi. He wrote: Owe officer money, can't pay back.

Then Niu Huhui. He wrote: Don't want to be sold.

Then Niu Lingang. He wrote: Being hunted.

Then Niu Guoming. He wrote: Mother needs medicine money.

The last entrant, Niu Heping. He wrote: Officer said either enter or die.

Eight slots, filled in less than an hour.

When the last entrant dropped his registration form into the wooden box, the overseer stood up, taking out a roll of red sealing tape from under the table. He stuck the tape on the lid of the wooden box; the wooden box was officially sealed.

"Ox Camp, eight people, full." the overseer announced. "Tomorrow at noon, the roll-call ceremony."

Niu Xuqiang stood in the crowd, looking at that sealed wooden box.

Eight people. Eight lives. Eight different predicaments—debt, family illness, being hunted, being forced. But all making the same choice—not because of bravery, but because there was no other way.

He clenched his fists, feeling the pain of nails digging into his palms.

That pain was real, certain, controllable. And on tomorrow's arena, what awaited him would be a much greater, much more uncontrollable pain—the kind that might end his life.

But at least, it was his own choice.

He turned around, following the other seven, slowly walking out of the administration building.

The morning fog had already begun to disperse; sunlight pierced through gaps in the clouds. Niu Xuqiang took a deep breath, letting the morning air fill his lungs.

Tomorrow, when the sun rose again, he would appear with a completely new identity—no longer E-217, no longer an ordinary soldier of the Ox Camp. But a contestant of the "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament," one of the eight oxen, a warrior fighting to survive.

He stepped forward, walking toward the barracks, toward that approaching, unknown fate.

## Scene-6: Where Eight Lives Meet

The next day, noon.

The sun hung high in the sky, emitting blinding white light. The air was filled with a dry, hot atmosphere, carrying the smell of metal and machine oil.

In the central square of the Ox Camp, three hundred E-level combat soldiers stood in neat formation. It was a spectacular sight—three hundred black figures, wearing uniform black uniforms, standing in neat square formations. In the noon sunlight, those black uniforms presented a deep, nearly ink-like quality.

Eight entrants stood at the very front of the formation.

Niu Xuqiang stood in the first position. His body was straight, his gaze level, like a stone statue. But beneath that still exterior, his heartbeat was faster than usual, his palms sweating.

Niu Sunyong stood in the second position. His breathing was steady, but the muscles of his neck were tense. His uniform was stretched full and tight, outlining the thick muscle contours beneath.

Niu Majun stood in the third position. The corner of his mouth carried a faint, almost imperceptible smile, fingers tapping lightly at the seams of his trousers, as if calculating some probability.

Niu Zhuzhi stood in the fourth position. His eyes were hollow, shoulders slightly hunched forward, forming a defensive posture. His uniform was old, collar and cuffs badly worn.

Niu Huhui stood in the fifth position. His back was ramrod straight, as if trying to prove at this final moment that he hadn't yet been crushed by years. His hands trembled slightly.

Niu Lingang stood in the sixth position. His body trembled slightly, but not from fear—from adrenaline. His uniform was new, forming an ironic contrast with his trembling body.

Niu Guoming stood in the seventh position. There was no expression on his face, only a deep exhaustion. His eyes had heavy dark circles beneath them.

Niu Heping stood in the eighth position. His gaze was cold, the corner of his mouth hanging with that half-smile expression. He was the youngest of the eight, only twenty-two.

"Eight confirmed." The overseer's voice was amplified through the loudspeaker, echoing across the square.

"Tomorrow at noon, the drawing ceremony."

The eight stood in place, like eight black statues.

Niu Xuqiang watched the other seven from the corner of his eye. They were all different—Niu Xuqiang for debt, Niu Sunyong for family, Niu Majun for the gamble, Niu Zhuzhi for release, Niu Huhui for dignity, Niu Lingang to survive, Niu Guoming for family love, Niu Heping because he had no choice.

Eight different predicaments, eight different reasons, eight different stories.

But they all had one thing in common: it wasn't that they had no fear, but that they had no way back.

These eight oxen represented eight different despairs. But on this battlefield, despair would become their most powerful weapon.

Because when a person has nothing left to lose, he becomes fearless.

Niu Xuqiang took a deep breath, letting that dry, hot air fill his lungs. In that instant, he felt a strange calm—accepting his situation, accepting the coming battle, accepting the possible arrival of death.

He only needed to face what was before him—tomorrow's drawing, the day after tomorrow's match, then the next match, until he fell or stood on the final podium.

That simplicity made his heart exceptionally calm.

Eight people stood in the square, eight sets of black uniforms gleaming with a matte finish in the sunlight, eight shadows stretching long on the ground. They were eight oxen, eight lives, eight reasons they had no choice but to fight.

And tomorrow, when the drawing ceremony began, their fates would be decided. They would know what kind of opponents they would face, what kind of battles they would experience, what kind of ending they would meet.

But before that, at this moment, they were equal—equal fear, equal despair, equal hope.

Niu Xuqiang raised his head, looking directly at the sun before him. The light stung his eyes, but he did not look away. He stared directly at that blinding light, as if challenging it, as if declaring his existence to it.

Because he knew that tomorrow's match would be hotter, crueler, more ruthless than this sunlight. If he couldn't endure this scorching, how could he face the slaughter on the arena?

His uniform grew darker and heavier from sweat soaking through. But his spine was still straight, his gaze still firm, his breathing still steady.

Because he was E-217, a warrior of the Ox Camp, a contestant of the "Blood-Bounty Elimination Tournament."

Because he was one of the eight oxen.

Because he was a man who had no choice but to fight.

Wind blew across the square, bringing a brief coolness. The wind carried this city's particular mixture of the scents of life and death.

Niu Xuqiang closed his eyes, letting that wind blow across his face. When he opened his eyes again, what he saw was still that square, those three hundred black figures, those seven companions standing beside him.

He was alive. He was standing. He was breathing.

That was enough. That was enough to be his reason to keep fighting.

No matter what awaited him tomorrow, no matter what opponent the drawing brought him, no matter whether the match ended in life or death, at this moment, he was one of the eight oxen, a warrior standing in the sunlight, a man who had no choice but to fight to survive.

This was him—E-217, Niu Xuqiang, thirty-two years old, owed the money house fifty-two thousand, the first of the eight oxen.

This was the path he chose. This was the path he had to walk. This was the path he had no choice but to walk.

No matter the outcome, at least, he had once been standing. At least, he had once been fighting. At least, he had once been alive.

That was enough. That was enough to let him straighten his spine, look straight ahead, and wait for tomorrow to come.
