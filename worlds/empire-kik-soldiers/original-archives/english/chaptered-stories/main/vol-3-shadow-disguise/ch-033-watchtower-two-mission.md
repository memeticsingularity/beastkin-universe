### Story Reflections

When I wrote B-11407's story, what I felt was a cold suffocation.

He lived for twenty days. Twenty days, from the cultivation pod to a snowy grave. He has no name, only a number. He has no dreams, only implanted orders. He hasn't even truly lived; he was just produced, used, and then scrapped as a tool.

But the cruelest thing is that in the 15% self-awareness suppressed by the chip, he might have had some fragments: a uniquely shaped pebble, the snowy mountain scenery on the eastern patrol route, the roll of bandages thrown by the squad leader. These fragments are not enough to constitute a "person," but they are enough to make his death not just the scrapping of a part, but the extinguishing of a faint life.

The world of "Long Live the Empire" is a precisely operating machine, and every Kik soldier is a gear within it. Gears don't need emotions, don't need memories, don't need a self. They only need to turn until they wear out, and then be replaced. But authors and readers are human; we cannot help but project humanity onto these beastmen. Thus, we see the rust on the gears, hear the whining of the bearings, and feel beneath those designed actions, the traces of life that could not be completely erased.

Hu Meng is being promoted, the snow leopard is pledging loyalty, and White Wolf is scheming. They seem "higher" than the black-sock soldiers, possessing more self-awareness. But isn't their self also a product carefully shaped by the chip and the hierarchy? Hu Meng's desire for power, the snow leopard's pursuit of recognition, White Wolf's obsession with returning to the center—these are all "desires" that the Empire allows them to have, because desires can be exploited and controlled.

In this world, freedom is the greatest taboo, and the self is the most dangerous virus. The Emperor has woven an inescapable net with chips, military socks, and physiological weaknesses, and every Kik soldier struggles within this net, yet thinks they are flying.

Why do I feel sad for B-11407? Because he didn't even have a chance to struggle. From the moment of his birth, his death was designed: either die in battle or be executed. His life is a simple math problem, and the answer is zero.

But perhaps, it is this absolute despair that constitutes the unique charm of "Long Live the Empire." It gives you no hope, no redemption, only naked domination and being dominated. It forces you to face a fact: in certain systems, life can really just be a consumable, and humanity can really be completely erased.

And that bit of reluctance, that wisp of sorrow we feel when reading, is a confirmation of our own humanity. Even in the darkest fictional world, we still cannot fully accept that life can be treated this way. This, perhaps, is the meaning of all tragic stories: in the fictional death of others, confirming that we ourselves are still alive.
