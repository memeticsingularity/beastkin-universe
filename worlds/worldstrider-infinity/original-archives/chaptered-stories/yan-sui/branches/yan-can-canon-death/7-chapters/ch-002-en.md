# Chapter 2 Porridge and Silence

Yan Shen didn't sleep all night.

He sat in that old chair, in the darkness, listening to the breathing sounds coming from the bedroom. That sound gradually changed from rapid to drawn out, occasionally mixed with a few vague murmurs, as if Suisui was having some bad dream. Every time a murmur rose, Yan Shen's fingers would tighten on the cane grip once, then slowly loosen.

When dawn was near, he stood up.

His left leg was stiff as a log from sitting too long; the joint at his knee made a few slight grinding sounds. He supported himself on the table, waiting for that numbness to recede bit by bit from his toes, then leaned on his cane, hobbling toward the kitchen.

The kitchen was very dark. Yan Shen didn't light the lamp—he didn't want light leaking through the door crack to wake Suisui. He just relied on memory, scooping out a small cup of rice from the rice jar, pouring it into the pot, adding water, then striking a match.

Blue flames licked at the bottom of the pot, making slight crackling sounds. Yan Shen sat on the small stool by the stove, watching that cluster of flames dance in the darkness. The firelight illuminated his face half bright half dark; white tiger whiskers gleamed with a soft silver-gray in the faint light.

The porridge slowly rolled in the pot, rice grains tumbling and expanding in the water, emitting a simple, warm aroma. Yan Shen stirred with a spoon, movements very slow, as if performing some ancient ritual.

A muffled sound came from the bedroom.

Yan Shen's hand paused. He listened with tilted ear, then stood up, leaning on his cane toward the bedroom.

Yan Sui was awake.

He was trying to turn over, but just tilted slightly when pain made him suck in a cold breath, his whole body freezing there, brows tightly furrowed together. His right hand still maintained the posture of trying to prop up the bed, but the muscles in his arm trembled slightly from the pain.

"...Don't move." Yan Shen said.

Yan Sui turned his head, looking at his father. His right eye was swollen into a narrow slit, but his left eye was still clear, carrying a just-awakened daze. His lips moved, voice hoarse: "...Dad... what time is it..."

"Still early." Yan Shen walked to the bedside, pressing Yan Sui back into the quilt, movements very light but unquestionable, "...Lie down."

Yan Sui didn't resist. He just followed his father's hand strength, slowly lying flat. But his brows were still furrowed, teeth lightly biting his lower lip—that was his habit of enduring pain, unchanged from childhood to adulthood.

Yan Shen lifted the quilt, checking Yan Sui's abdomen.

That bruise from last night looked even more shocking under the morning light. Purplish-red blood congestion spread from below the ribs all the way to the side waist, the edges showing an unhealthy greenish-yellow. Yan Shen gently pressed with his finger; Yan Sui's body immediately bounced, a broken whimper overflowing from his throat.

"...Hurts?"

"...A little..." Yan Sui's voice was very light, like he was lying.

Yan Shen didn't expose him. He just re-covered the quilt, then said: "...Porridge is ready. I'll bring it."

He turned and walked toward the kitchen, left leg dragging on the ground, making slight friction sounds. That sound was especially clear in the morning silence, like some old machine struggling to operate.

Yan Shen brought a bowl of white porridge. The porridge was cooked very thick, rice grains blooming, sprinkled with a little chopped pickled vegetable, and a few drops of sesame oil. Very simple, but in this worn-down room, that aroma gave a near-painful comfort.

He sat on the edge of the bed, using a spoon to scoop up a mouthful, blew on it, and brought it to Yan Sui's lips.

"...I'll do it myself..." Yan Sui wanted to take the bowl, but his arm had just lifted when pain made him hiss, falling back.

"...I'll feed you." Yan Shen said.

Yan Sui didn't insist anymore. He followed his father's hand, drinking the porridge one mouthful at a time. His movements were very light, very careful, as if performing some precious ritual. With every drink, he would pause, swallow, then wait for the next mouthful.

The room was very quiet. Only the slight sound of the spoon touching the bowl edge, and the distant morning noise unique to White Capital coming from outside the window—the garbage truck's rumbling, the morning shift factory's steam whistle, and faint voices of people.

"...Dad." Yan Sui suddenly said.

"Mm."

"...Sorry."

Yan Shen's hand paused. The porridge spoon hung in midair; steam rose curling, rapidly dissipating in the morning cold air.

"...I shouldn't have gone out..." Yan Sui's voice was very low, carrying a self-blame unique to young people who thought themselves mature, "...I shouldn't have taken that road... shouldn't have let them see..."

"...Eat porridge." Yan Shen said.

"...Dad, I—"

"...Eat porridge." Yan Shen repeated, voice still very light, but with something more—not severity, but an exhausted, unquestionable firmness.

Yan Sui closed his mouth. He lowered his head, following his father's hand, finishing the remaining porridge.

Yan Shen put down the bowl, stood up, beginning to tidy the things on the nightstand. He straightened the water cup, folded Yan Sui's changed dirty clothes and placed them aside, then—his hand stopped.

The nightstand drawer wasn't closed tight, revealing a corner of yellowed paper.

Yan Shen used two fingers to gently pull out that stack of papers. That was a stack of handwritten "communication coupons," the paper already yellowed and brittle, edges worn fuzzy by time. The handwriting on them was from many years ago, written by Yan Shen, stroke by stroke, crooked, but every character written very seriously:

"With this coupon, Dad will definitely talk properly with Suisui no matter what."

Yan Shen looked at that stack of paper, looked for a long time.

He recalled many years ago, when Suisui was still little, because of a misunderstanding, Suisui thought Dad didn't want him anymore, and ran away from home alone. Yan Shen searched the entire western district, finally finding Suisui curled beside a trash can in a back alley. That night, he carried Suisui home, crying as he walked—that was the only time he cried before his son in his life.

After returning home, he hand-wrote a large stack of such communication coupons. He said to Suisui: "In the future, no matter what happens, as long as you give this coupon to Dad, Dad will definitely stop and listen to you properly, talk properly with you. Dad won't let you be alone again."

At that time Suisui was only five years old. He treasured that stack of communication coupons like treasure under his pillow, counting them every night before willing to sleep.

Now that paper was still there. But the person who wrote the paper, and the person who collected the paper, were no longer their former selves.

Yan Shen put the communication coupons back in place, gently pushing the drawer closed.

"...Dad?" Yan Sui was looking at him.

"...Mm?"

"...I have something I want to tell you." Yan Sui's voice was somewhat hesitant, "...About those people last night... about my work..."

Yan Shen turned around, looking at his son.

Yan Sui's gaze was very serious, even solemn. His fingers unconsciously rubbed on the quilt, as if organizing his words. That expression Yan Shen was very familiar with—whenever Suisui had some important decision, he would have such an expression.

"...Wait until Dad comes back to talk." Yan Shen said.

Yan Sui was stunned: "...You're going out?"

"...Mm." Yan Shen began to pack things, taking out a thicker overcoat from the cabinet, "...Go find someone."

"...Who?"

"...Lao Zhao." Yan Shen draped the overcoat on his body, movements somewhat clumsy from his left leg's inconvenience, "...Former Beastshield old colleague."

Yan Sui's brow furrowed. He wanted to say something—perhaps "don't go," perhaps "I'll go with you"—but the pain in his body prevented him from organizing his thoughts. He just opened his one intact eye, looking at his father.

Yan Shen read that gaze.

He walked back to the bedside, reached out, gently, clumsily, rubbing the top of Yan Sui's head. That was his way of expressing comfort—not good at saying "it's okay," not good at saying "don't worry," only able to use this clumsy physical contact to convey his feelings.

"...Lock the door." he said, "...Besides me, don't open for anyone."

"...Mm."

"...Porridge is in the pot; heat it yourself when hungry."

"...Mm."

Yan Shen looked at his son once more. That look was very long, as if trying to etch Yan Sui's appearance into his eyes. Then he turned around, leaning on his cane, hobbling out of the bedroom.

The door closed.

Yan Sui lay in the quilt, listening to his father's footsteps gradually fading in the living room, then the sound of the front door opening and closing. That sound was very light, but in this quiet morning, it was like a heavy sigh.

---

Morning in White Capital, even the air was gray.

Yan Shen leaned on his cane, walking on the snow-covered streets. The sky was already bright, but the sun was blocked by thick coal smoke clouds; only a bit of pale white light leaked through the cloud gaps, shining on the dirty snow, making the whole world look like a faded old photograph.

His left leg was especially stiff in the morning cold. Every step, the old wound at his knee seemed to remind him: you're old, you're disabled, you're no longer that Yan Shen who could run ten laps on the Beastshield training ground without panting.

Clack. Clack.

The cane tapping the ground echoed on the empty streets. There weren't many pedestrians on the road; occasionally a few civilians in worn cotton-padded clothes hurried past. When they saw Yan Shen's figure, they would subconsciously slow their steps, or detour to the other side of the street.

Yan Shen noticed. But he didn't react. He just continued walking forward.

An old woman pushing a cart passed by him. The cart was piled with empty bottles and waste paper, making clinking sounds. The old woman glanced at Yan Shen; in her eyes was a complex emotion—not fear, but something deeper. Perhaps pity? Perhaps fellow sufferers? Everyone in White Capital had their own suffering; who had the qualification to pity whom?

Yan Shen continued walking.

The buildings on both sides of the street grew taller and more dilapidated. Apartment buildings with peeling wall paint, abandoned shops with windows boarded up with wooden planks, and those mottled slogans on the walls—some were half-torn Beastshield propaganda posters, some were graffiti sprayed by unknown people. A skinny stray dog rummaged for food beside a garbage pile; hearing the cane sound, it raised its head, looking at Yan Shen with a pair of turbid eyes, then continued rummaging.

Yan Shen stopped beneath a broken streetlamp.

He needed to rest. His left leg had already begun to ache dully; that pain wasn't a sharp stab, but a dull, bone-deep ache, like countless ants crawling in the joint. He leaned against the lamppost, taking out a water flask from his bosom, drinking a small mouthful. The water was cold, carrying a rusty taste, but he still swallowed it.

He raised his head, looking ahead.

Lao Zhao lived at the edge of the western district, in an old apartment building blackened by coal smoke. From there, walk two more streets, and you could see an abandoned factory—that was where they trained at Beastshield in their youth, now already turned into ruins.

Yan Shen didn't know if Lao Zhao could help. Honestly, he also didn't know what he wanted from going to find Lao Zhao. Did he want Lao Zhao to come forward to mediate? Did he want to borrow some money to take Suisui away from White Capital? Or did he merely want an old comrade to keep him company, to talk, so he wouldn't completely collapse in this icy morning?

He didn't know.

But he knew one thing: he couldn't just sit and wait for death. That group hadn't gotten their way last night; they wouldn't let it go. Suisui was still young, still had a future. And he, this old man—this old man who could barely walk—could at least do one last thing for his son.

Even if it meant begging an old comrade he hadn't seen for years.

Yan Shen straightened up, stuffed the water flask back into his bosom, leaned on his cane, and continued walking forward.

Wind blew from the alley, carrying a mixed smell of rot and coal smoke. Yan Shen pulled his collar up higher, covering half his face. His breath turned into puffs of white mist in the cold air; every pant carried a heavy exhaustion.

But he didn't stop.

At the end of the street ahead, that old apartment building blackened by coal smoke was already faintly visible. Yan Shen stared in that direction, his gaze very calm, but the hand holding the cane tightened slightly.

He didn't know what awaited him.

But he knew he must go.

(End of Chapter Two)
