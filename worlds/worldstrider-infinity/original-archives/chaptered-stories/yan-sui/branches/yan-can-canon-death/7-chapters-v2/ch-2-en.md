# Chapter 2 Porridge and Silence

Yan Shen didn't sleep all night.

He sat in that old chair, in the darkness, listening to the breathing sounds coming from the bedroom. That sound gradually changed from rapid to long and steady, occasionally mixed with a few vague murmurs, like Suisui was having some bad dream. Every time a murmur sounded, Yan Shen's fingers would tighten on the cane grip once, then slowly loosen.

When dawn was near, he stood up.

His left leg was stiff as a log from sitting too long, the joint at the knee making a few slight friction sounds. He supported himself on the table, waiting for that numbness to recede bit by bit from his toes, then leaned on his cane, hobbling toward the kitchen.

The kitchen was very dark. Yan Shen didn't light the lamp—he didn't want light leaking through the door crack to wake Suisui. He just relied on memory, scooping a small cup of rice from the rice jar, pouring it into the pot, adding water, then striking a match.

Blue flames licked the bottom of the pot, making slight crackling sounds. Yan Shen sat on the small stool by the stove, watching that cluster of flames jump in the darkness. The firelight illuminated his face half-bright half-dark, white tiger whiskers showing a soft silver-gray in the dim light.

The porridge slowly boiled in the pot, rice grains tumbling and expanding in the water, emitting a simple, warm aroma. Yan Shen stirred with the spoon, movements very slow, like performing some ancient ritual.

A muffled sound came from the bedroom.

Yan Shen's hand paused. He listened with his ear, then stood up, leaning on his cane toward the bedroom.

Yan Sui was awake.

He was trying to turn over, but just as he turned a bit, he sucked in a cold breath of pain, his whole person freezing there, brow tightly furrowed together. His right hand still maintained the posture of trying to prop up the bed, but the muscles in his arm trembled slightly from the pain.

"...Don't move." Yan Shen said.

Yan Sui turned his head, looking at his father. His right eye was swollen to only a slit, but his left eye was still clear, carrying a just-awakened confusion. His lips moved, voice hoarse: "...Dad... what time is it..."

"Still early." Yan Shen walked to the bedside, pressing Yan Sui back into the quilt, movements very light but unquestionable, "...Lie down."

Yan Sui didn't resist. He just followed his father's hand strength, slowly lying flat. But his brow was still furrowed, teeth lightly biting his lower lip—that was his habit when enduring pain, never changed from childhood to adulthood.

Yan Shen lifted the quilt, checking Yan Sui's abdomen.

That bruise from last night looked even more shocking under the morning light. Purplish-red blood congestion spread from below the ribs all the way to the side waist, edges showing an unhealthy green-yellow color. Yan Shen lightly pressed with his finger, Yan Sui's body immediately bounced, a broken whimper escaping from his throat.

"...Hurts?"

"...A bit..." Yan Sui's voice was very light, like he was lying.

Yan Shen didn't expose him. He just re-covered the quilt, then said: "...Porridge is ready. I'll bring it."

He turned and walked toward the kitchen, left leg dragging on the ground, making slight friction sounds. That sound was exceptionally clear in the morning silence, like some old machine struggling to operate.

Yan Shen brought a bowl of white porridge. The porridge was cooked very thick, rice grains blooming, topped with a bit of chopped pickled vegetables, and a few drops of sesame oil. Very simple, but in this broken-down room, that aroma gave a nearly painful comfort.

He sat on the edge of the bed, scooping a spoonful with the spoon, blowing on it, bringing it to Yan Sui's mouth.

"...I'll do it myself..." Yan Sui wanted to take the bowl, but just as his arm lifted, he hissed in pain and fell back.

"...I'll feed you." Yan Shen said.

Yan Sui didn't insist further. He drank the porridge from his father's hand, mouthful by mouthful. His movements were very light, very careful, like performing some precious ritual. Every drink, he would pause, swallow, then wait for the next mouthful.

The room was very quiet. Only the slight sound of the spoon hitting the bowl rim, and the distant noise unique to White Capital's early morning outside the window—the garbage truck's rumble, the morning shift factory's steam whistle, and faint voices of people.

"...Dad." Yan Sui suddenly said.

"Mm."

"...Sorry."

Yan Shen's hand paused. The porridge spoon hung in midair, steam rising, rapidly dissipating in the cold morning air.

"...I shouldn't have gone out..." Yan Sui's voice was very low, carrying a young person's characteristic, self-assumed mature self-blame, "...I shouldn't have taken that road... shouldn't have let them see..."

"...Eat porridge." Yan Shen said.

"...Dad, I—"

"...Eat porridge." Yan Shen repeated, voice still very light, but with something more—not sternness, but an exhausted, unquestionable firmness.

Yan Sui closed his mouth. He lowered his head, drinking the remaining porridge from his father's hand.

Yan Shen put down the bowl, stood up, beginning to tidy the things on the nightstand. He straightened the water cup, folded Yan Sui's changed dirty clothes and placed them aside, then—his hand stopped.

The nightstand drawer wasn't closed tight, revealing a corner of yellowed paper.

Yan Shen used two fingers to gently pull that stack of paper out. That was a stack of handwritten "communication coupons," paper already yellowed and brittle, edges worn fuzzy by time. The handwriting on it was Yan Shen's from many years ago, stroke by stroke, crooked, but every word written very seriously:

"With this coupon, Dad will definitely talk properly with Suisui no matter what."

Yan Shen looked at that stack of paper, looked for a very long time.

He remembered many years ago, when Suisui was still small, because of a misunderstanding, Suisui thought Dad didn't want him anymore, and ran away from home alone. Yan Shen searched the entire western district, finally finding Suisui curled beside a trash can in a back alley. That night, he carried Suisui home, crying as he walked—that was the only time in his life he cried before his son.

After returning home, he hand-wrote a large stack of such communication coupons. He said to Suisui: "In the future no matter what happens, as long as you give this coupon to Dad, Dad will definitely stop, listen to you properly, talk properly with you. Dad won't let you be alone again."

Back then Suisui was only five. He treasured that stack of communication coupons like a treasure, hiding them under his pillow, counting them every night before he was willing to sleep.

Now that paper was still there. But the person who wrote it, and the person who collected it, were no longer their former selves.

Yan Shen put the communication coupons back in place, gently pushing the drawer closed.

"...Dad?" Yan Sui looked at him.

"...Mm?"

"...I have something I want to tell you." Yan Sui's voice was somewhat hesitant, "...About those people last night... about my work..."

Yan Shen turned around, looking at his son.

Yan Sui's gaze was very serious, even solemn. His fingers unconsciously rubbed on the quilt, like organizing language. That expression Yan Shen was very familiar with—whenever Suisui had some important decision, he would have such an expression.

"...Wait until Dad comes back." Yan Shen said.

Yan Sui was stunned: "...You're going out?"

"...Mm." Yan Shen began packing things, pulling out a thicker overcoat from the cabinet, "...Going to find someone."

"...Who?"

"...Yi An." Yan Shen draped the overcoat over his body, movements somewhat clumsy from his left leg's inconvenience, "...A former Beastshield junior."

Yan Sui's brow furrowed. He wanted to say something—perhaps "don't go," perhaps "I'll go with you"—but bodily pain prevented him from organizing language. He just opened his one good eye, looking at his father.

Yan Shen read that gaze.

He walked back to the bedside, reached out, lightly, clumsily, rubbing the top of Yan Sui's head. That was his way of expressing comfort—not good at saying "it's fine," not good at saying "don't worry," only able to use this clumsy physical contact to convey his feelings.

"...Lock the door." he said, "...Don't open for anyone except me."

"...Mm."

"...Porridge is in the pot, heat it yourself when hungry."

"...Mm."

Yan Shen looked at his son once more. That glance was very long, like he wanted to etch Yan Sui's appearance into his eyes. Then he turned around, leaning on his cane, hobbling out of the bedroom.

The door closed.

Yan Sui lay in the quilt, listening to his father's footsteps gradually fading away in the living room, then the front door opening and closing. That sound was very light, but in this silent morning, it was like a heavy sigh.

---

White Capital in the early morning, even the air was gray.

Yan Shen leaned on his cane, walking on the snow-covered streets. The sky was already bright, but the sun was blocked by thick coal smoke clouds, only a bit of bleak white light leaking through the cloud cracks, shining on the dirty snow, making the whole world look like a faded old photograph.

His left leg was exceptionally stiff in the morning cold. Every step, the old wound at the knee seemed to remind him: you're old, you're crippled, you're no longer that Yan Shen who could run ten laps on the Beastshield training ground without panting.

Thud. Thud.

The cane struck the ground, echoing on the empty streets. There weren't many pedestrians on the road, occasionally a few civilians in worn cotton-padded clothes hurried past. When they saw Yan Shen's figure, they would subconsciously slow their steps, or cross to the other side of the street.

Yan Shen noticed. But he didn't react. He just continued walking forward.

An old woman pushing a cart passed by him. The cart was piled with empty bottles and waste paper, making clanging sounds. The old woman glanced at Yan Shen, that gaze carrying a complex emotion—not fear, but something deeper. Perhaps pity? Perhaps fellow sufferers? Everyone in White Capital had their own suffering, who had the right to pity whom?

Yan Shen continued walking.

The buildings on both sides of the street grew taller and more broken-down. Apartment buildings with peeling wall paint, abandoned shops with windows boarded up with wooden planks, and those mottled slogans on the walls—some were half-torn Beastshield propaganda posters, some were graffiti sprayed by who-knows-who. A bony stray dog rummaged for food beside a garbage pile, looking up when it heard the cane sound, using a pair of turbid eyes to glance at Yan Shen, then continuing to rummage with its head down.

Yan Shen stopped under a broken street lamp.

He needed to rest. His left leg was already starting to hurt dully, that pain not a sharp stabbing, but a dull, seeping ache from deep in the bones, like countless ants crawling in the joints. He leaned against the lamp post, pulling out a water flask from his chest, drinking a small sip. The water was cold, carrying a rusty taste, but he still swallowed it.

He raised his head, looking ahead.

Yi An lived nearby, in an old apartment building two streets away. Yan Shen had been there a few times—during holidays, that young man would always bring things to visit him, sit a while, listen to him talk about the past. Yi An was a junior he had personally promoted when he was still at Beastshield, a Bernese Mountain Dog, now wearing a blue uniform, having some weight in White Capital's bottom-level system.

Yan Shen usually didn't want to disturb this young man. Firstly, he didn't want people to think he was leaning on his seniority, secondly... he wasn't used to asking juniors for help. But tonight was different. That group wouldn't let it go, Suisui was still young, still had a future. And this old man—this old man who could barely walk—could at least do one last thing for his son.

Even if it meant going to beg a junior he had personally promoted.

Yan Shen straightened up, stuffing the water flask back into his chest, leaning on his cane, continuing to walk forward.

Wind blew out from the alley, carrying a mixed smell of rot and coal smoke. Yan Shen pulled his collar up higher, covering half his face. His breath turned into puffs of white mist in the cold air, every pant carrying heavy exhaustion.

But he didn't stop.

At the end of the street ahead, that old apartment was already faintly visible. A light was on in the third-floor window—Yi An always woke very early, probably preparing for patrol. Yan Shen stared in that direction, his gaze very calm, but the hand holding the cane slightly tightened.

He didn't know what awaited him.

But he knew he had to go.

(End of Chapter Two)
