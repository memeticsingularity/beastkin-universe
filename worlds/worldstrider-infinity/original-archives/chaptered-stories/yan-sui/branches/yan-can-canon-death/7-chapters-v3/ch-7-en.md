# Chapter 7 No Return

When Yi An walked home, the snow had grown lighter.

Not stopped, but that kind of feeble, like the last struggle before something is about to be exhausted. Snowflakes changed from dense to sparse, from goose feather to fine powder, finally leaving only scattered few pieces, drifting in the dawn's blue light, unable to find a place to land.

His apartment was on another street, a bit farther than Yan Shen's home, and older. The hallway light was broken; he groped his way up to the third floor in darkness, key turning half a circle in the lock, door opening.

The room was very cold. The stove fire had long gone out.

Yi An stood in the doorway, not entering immediately. He just stood in the frame, looking at the darkness inside. That bed—that bed Yan Shen had just lain on—stood silently in the corner, the bedsheet still retaining a human-shaped depression, quilt piled messily to one side.

He walked in, closed the door.

Didn't light the lamp. He didn't need light. He walked to the stove, picked up the iron tongs, scattering the embers in the stove ash bit by bit. Sparks brightened in the darkness for a moment, then dimmed again. The last bit of warmth dissipated in the air, leaving only the cold iron stove wall and a faint smell of coal ash.

Yi An sat on the edge of the bed.

The bedsheet still retained a bit of warmth. Very faint, about to dissipate soon. He pressed his palm flat on that depression, feeling that last bit of heat seeping up from beneath the fabric, seeping into his palm.

No other sound. Only his own breathing, and the gradually quieting wind outside the window.

His ears slowly—very slowly—perked up from the sides of his head. His tail loosened from between his legs, hanging by the edge of the bed, motionless.

He accepted.

---

When Yan Sui woke up, sunlight was shining in through the curtain crack.

It was a very thin beam, slanting across the bedroom wall, landing on the floor at the foot of the bed, turning the floating dust in the air into golden particles. Yan Sui's eyes squinted from that beam, then he turned over, burying his face in the pillow.

His body was very heavy. Every place that had been beaten was faintly aching, like a swarm of small bugs crawling under the skin. But he slept very well—surprisingly well, like someone had cast some magic, pulling him out from pain, placing him in some warm and safe place.

He opened his eyes, looking at the ceiling.

The room was very quiet. Too quiet. No sound of noodle soup boiling in the pot, no thud-thud of cane tapping cement, no slight rustle of father moving quietly in the kitchen.

Yan Sui sat up.

His movement pulled at the abdominal bruise, making him suck in a breath of pain. But he didn't care. He just sat on the bed, listening to the silence in the room. That silence had weight, pressing on his eardrums, pressing on his chest, making him unable to breathe.

"...Dad?"

He called out. Voice very light, carrying the hoarseness of just waking up.

No answer.

Yan Sui lifted the quilt, walking off the bed barefoot. The floor was cold, but he couldn't feel it. He walked to the bedroom door, pushed it open.

The living room was very bright. Sunlight poured in from the east window, whitening everything. Coffee table, chairs, sofa—

On the sofa lay a person.

Yan Sui's footsteps stopped.

He looked at that person. Gray-and-white fur, military-green old uniform, hands crossed over abdomen, expression calm. Sunlight shone on that person's face, white tiger whiskers gleaming with soft silver light in the rays, like covered with a thin layer of frost.

"...Dad?"

Yan Sui's voice was even lighter than before. Light like afraid of startling something.

He walked over. One step, two steps. His left leg was somewhat lame—the afterpain from yesterday's kicking still remained—but he walked very steadily. He walked to the sofa side, squatting down.

Yan Shen's eyes were closed. Eyelashes cast a small patch of shadow on his pale cheek. The corner of his mouth carried a trace of very faint arc, like having a peaceful dream.

Yan Sui reached out, touching Yan Shen's face.

Cold.

Not the cold of ice, but that kind of... temperature-losing cold. Like hot water placed outside the window in winter, still emitting faint white steam, but no longer hot.

Yan Sui's finger moved to below Yan Shen's nose.

No breathing.

His hand paused. Like frozen in midair by something. His eyes opened very wide, pupils contracting into pinpoints in the morning light. His lips moved, like wanting to say something, but no sound came out.

His gaze moved to the coffee table.

There was a piece of paper there. A folded, uneven-edged paper. Under the paper was a small paper packet; below that—he recognized it—a stack of crumpled paper slips, edges fuzzy, with father's crooked handwriting.

Communication vouchers.

Yan Sui's hand trembled. He picked up that paper, unfolding it.

"Suisui. I am your father's junior. Your father has passed. Died of illness. He passed very—"

The last word was crossed out. One stroke, two strokes, three strokes. Black ink smeared that word into a blurry blot, like a small, black wound.

The paper slipped from Yan Sui's hand, floating down onto the coffee table, landing beside that packet of styptic powder.

Yan Sui didn't look at it. He only turned his head, looking at Yan Shen again. Looking at that calm face, at that old uniform, at those crossed hands.

He gripped Yan Shen's hand.

That hand was smaller than in his memory. Thin. Bones protruding, knuckles stiff. But still retained a bit of warmth—the last bit, like an oil lamp about to burn out.

Yan Sui raised that hand, pressing it against his own forehead.

His forehead pressed against the back of Yan Shen's hand. That hand's warmth seeped through skin, into his forehead, into his skull, into some place he couldn't name.

He didn't cry.

He only knelt there, forehead against the back of his father's hand, like when he was little lying on his father's back. His shoulders didn't shake; no sound came from his throat. Only his tail—that orange-yellow tiger tail—hung on the floor, motionless.

Outside the window, the snow had stopped.

Sunlight shone on them. On Yan Shen's gray-and-white fur, on Yan Sui's light golden fur, on that note with the last word crossed out, on that stack of fuzzy-edged communication vouchers at the edge of the coffee table.

The room was very quiet.

No legends, no dreams, no stars.

Only sunlight, and the warmth on the back of a hand.
