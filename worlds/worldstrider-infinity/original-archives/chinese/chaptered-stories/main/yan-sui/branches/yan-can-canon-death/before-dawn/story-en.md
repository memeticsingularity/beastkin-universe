# Before Dawn

My name is Lao Zhao. Former Beastshield soldier, now a retired old man, living in a broken apartment building in White Capital's western district, bad waist, bad knees, every winter feeling like I might not make it through. But I'm still alive.

Yan Shen is dead. Two years ago today, he died on this bed.

I've never been certain whether what I did was right or not. These two years I've thought about it countless times, every time from the beginning, every detail pondered over and over. But I'm still not certain. Perhaps when a person reaches my age, they should understand that some things in this world simply have no right or wrong.

---

That night I was originally preparing to turn off the light and sleep.

Every night before bed I do the same routine: add coal to the stove, prepare the next day's dry rations, check the door lock. Getting old, memory not good, always have to confirm repeatedly several times before feeling at ease. I was about to turn off the light when I heard someone knocking at the door.

It was Lao Zhou. He was wrapped in an old overcoat, standing in the doorway, the expression on his face making my heart sink.

"Lao Zhao," he said, "how long since you've seen Yan Shen?"

Yan Shen. I hadn't seen him for some time. His leg wasn't good, my waist wasn't good; two old fellows wanting to get together, every time either he hurt too much to get out of bed or I felt too cold to go out.

"Over two weeks."

Lao Zhou was silent for a moment, then told me something. Yan Shen was missing. Half a month ago he went to find an old friend; after that, no one had seen him again.

My hands were trembling. Lao Zhou said, "I also just heard that some time ago someone saw a tiger beastkin being dragged into an abandoned warehouse building in the western district's back alley." My hands kept trembling while dressing, not from cold, but a bad premonition drilling out from the marrow of my bones.

Lao Zhou took me to that alley. I recognized that alley; even in daytime it was as dark as dusk, ground covered with years-old coal cinders and frozen snow mud. We stopped before a rusted iron door. Lao Zhou said he had to go contact Beastshield people to handle it, let me go in first.

I pushed open the iron door, walked in.

That was a downward staircase, very steep, very dark. I supported myself on the wall, walking down step by step. The further down I went, the stronger that smell became. Not mold, not garbage stench, but something more complex—the fishy smell of blood, the fishy smell of people, and an indescribable smell belonging to fear and despair.

Pushing open the door at the end of the stairs, I saw the most unbearable sight I had ever seen in my life.

In the basement, only a dim yellow light bulb was swaying, illuminating things in the room in flickering light and shadow. On the ground were water stains, dried bloodstains, and some things I didn't want to look at closely. In the center of the room was an iron pipe; tied to the iron pipe was an iron chain, and at the other end of the chain was a collar.

Yan Shen was curled at the other end of the chain.

He leaned against the wall, knees hugged to his chest, naked upper body full of wounds. Some wounds had already scabbed, some were still oozing pus. Gray-white fur was covered with dirt and bloodstains. The iron chain hung from the collar on his neck to the ground; the length was only enough for him to move within about two meters.

I walked in. He heard the footsteps, but he didn't raise his head. His body instinctively shrank toward the wall corner, hands slightly protecting his head, as if long accustomed to this posture. A very light whimper came from his throat—not a cry for help, but begging for mercy.

"Yan Shen."

My voice made him stiffen. Then he slowly raised his head. His eyes were turbid; after quite a while they focused on my face.

"...Lao Zhao?"

"It's me."

He cried. Not wailing loudly, but a muffled, broken sound coming from his nasal cavity, then his body began to tremble violently, tears gushing from his eye rims, washing out rows of white skin traces on the dirt.

I told him not to move, then went up to undo that collar. It was stuck very tight; the leather surface had worn the skin of his neck, the strangulation mark deep purple. I pried for quite a while before prying the buckle open; the leather surface was stuck together with his flesh, and when I pulled it apart he gritted his teeth from the pain, but didn't cry out. The collar fell to the ground, emitting a dull sound. Then I undid the rope on his wrists.

"Stand up," I said.

But he couldn't stand up. That left leg was already swollen like a section of blackened old tree trunk, completely unable to bear weight. I could only support him, half-carrying, half-dragging, getting him out of that basement step by step.

---

After bringing him home, the first thing was to boil hot water.

I placed him on the bed. He curled there, body constantly trembling. I brought a basin of water, using a towel to wipe his body. Starting from the face. His face was full of blood scabs and mud; I discovered a still-bleeding gash at the temple. His body was much thinner than in my memory; ribs floated one by one beneath the fur, like indentations on a stone slab. The towel moved downward, his arms, chest, abdomen, every inch of skin had wounds. Wounds upon wounds, everywhere old wounds.

What made me most speechless was the neck. That strangulation mark was too deep; the skin had already festered, emitting a fishy smell all around.

When I applied medicine to him, he didn't speak the whole time. Only at a certain instant, when my hand touched that old wound knee of his, he emitted a very light, suppressed gasp.

"Hurts?" I asked.

"It's fine." he said.

This was the first sentence he spoke after returning to my place. Later I learned what his "it's fine" meant—not that it didn't hurt, but that this bit of pain compared to before was nothing.

After I finished, I sat by the bedside, wanting to find something to say. But he spoke first.

"Lao Zhao."

"Mm."

"I don't want to talk yet."

"Alright," I said, "you sleep first, talk after you wake up."

He closed his eyes. I guarded him there the whole night, fire dying and rekindling, rekindling and dying, outside the window changing from pitch black to gray-white. He slept very deeply, also very unsteadily. Woke up in fright several times in between, trembling all over each time, only relaxing after seeing me.

When dawn came, he woke up.

"Where's Suisui." he said.

"I don't know," I said, "Lao Zhou will handle it. You recover first."

He didn't speak. He got up and sat on the edge of the bed, head lowered, looking at those hands placed on his knees. Those hands were thin like withered branches; nails still had dirt and dried blood traces.

Then he said, "Lao Zhao, help me with something."

"What thing."

"Kill me."

The water cup in my hand shook; water spilled out in a splash.

"What did you say?"

He repeated it. Voice not loud, every word bitten very clearly.

I looked at him. His gaze was very calm; that was something I had never seen before—not despair, despair is still alive. The layer of gray in his eyes was dead, like having already given up all struggle, only waiting for a confirmation.

"You're crazy."

"I'm not crazy, I'm very clear."

"Clear my ass. You still have a son! What about Suisui? What will he do?"

The weight of those two words made him quiet. In the room, only the sound of flames jumping in the stove remained. After a very long time, he spoke.

"In my present state, I can't go back to see Suisui."

"You can go back after recovering—"

"Recover?" He raised his head looking at me, the corner of his mouth slightly pulling—that wasn't a smile. "I can't recover, Lao Zhao. Wounds can heal, some things can't."

He lowered his head, slowly saying, "They forced me to eat on the ground, eat those things covered with mud. They dragged the iron chain walking me, made me kneel and crawl over to receive with my mouth. I resisted; in the first two days I still fought back, they knocked me over on the ground, stepped on that old wound knee of mine, I could hear my own bones cracking."

His voice was very flat, like telling a story that had nothing to do with himself.

"Then I learned," he said, "because I discovered resistance only made me suffer more. I learned to kneel down when hearing commands, learned to please them, learned to instinctively curl into a ball trembling when hearing footsteps at the threshold. At first it was forced out, later it wasn't."

I couldn't say a single word.

"Suisui will see," he said, "sooner or later he'll discover I had already curled into a ball before he called my name. He'll discover the wounds on my body were never accidents, he'll know there was once a collar on here, being led around by someone. When he asks people for directions on the street, what flashes through his mind is his father being chained on the ground trembling. How do you expect him to live his life?"

"I'd rather let him remember a bastard who died on a winter night than let him know his father was an old dog chained in the corner." When he said this, his voice finally began to tremble, eye rims red, but didn't let tears fall.

The room was very quiet. I listened to the sound of flames in the stove, listened to wind passing through the broken streetlamp outside the window. After a very long time, I spoke.

"What do you want me to do."

"You know," he said, "in the torture room, that least painful one."

I closed my eyes. I knew what he meant. There was a destined passage in our bodies; a full semen gauge would lead a person to that final peace. On the battlefield, those who stayed behind to collect bodies had seen it—some people still had a trace of smile at the corner of their mouths.

"Are you certain."

"Certain."

I stood before the window for a very long time. Dawn was near; gray-white morning light like a thin sheet of ice reflected on the window. After dawn, I might not be able to bring myself to do it.

"Alright." I said.

---

I didn't act immediately.

I first wiped his body. This time it wasn't cleaning wounds. The dirt on his body was already wiped clean, but I wanted to let him leave cleanly.

I brought a basin of warm water, had him take off his clothes and sit on the edge of the bed, using a towel to carefully scrub his back. He was even quieter than before washing, eyes closed, as if finally waiting for this moment. Then I searched through the cabinet for that set of Beastshield old uniform only worn once. That was something he gave me when he transferred, saying he couldn't wear it anymore, giving it to me as a memento.

"Put it on."

He put it on. That set of clothes was a whole size too big for him, but the shoulder badge was still there, the belt cinched the waist very tight.

He lowered his head looking at himself, then nodded.

"You look most proper wearing this."

His eye rims reddened again, but sucked in a breath and held it back. Then he leaned back against the bed, quilt pulled to his chest. Outside the window, the sky was already slightly bright; gray-white light reflected on the window glass, setting off his thin outline.

"Smoke this cigarette," I handed him the last cigarette in my pocket, "as a ritual."

He took it, held it in his mouth. I lit it for him. He took a drag, exhaled a thin wisp of white smoke, then lightly smiled. That smile was very faint, but more like the Yan Shen in my memory than any expression I saw in the basement.

"Do it," he said, "it's almost dawn."

I reached my hand into the quilt.

His body slightly stiffened under my touch, then relaxed like releasing strength. I still remember the expression on that face—from the basement's panic and unease to the exhausted resignation when I cleaned him, in those few minutes it became a certain kind of relief. Not smiling, but relaxed. Relaxed, like about to start a dream.

He only said two sentences before dying.

One was: "Still be brothers in the next life."

One was: "Don't let him know."

Then his body tensed for an instant, exhaled a long breath, then slowly fell back onto the bed, falling into that morning's gray light.

I didn't withdraw that hand.

I just sat like that for a very long time, watching him quietly fall asleep. The sky was already bright; the morning shift steam whistle on the street sounded its first note. My tears only came down then, dripping on the quilt before him.

---

Later I carried him home.

By then the sky was completely bright. His left leg dragged on the ground; I took one step, his left leg slid on the ground once. I still remember that very light, very slow scraping to this day, like a cane dragging on snow. Entering the door, I placed him on the bed. The way he lay down there was heavier than on the way back.

I wiped him a second time; this time, it was for dignity. The towel inch by inch slid over his skin that had already lost temperature, wiping clean the last body, combing hair and sparse eyebrows. Then I searched through his wardrobe and found an old-style military-green underwear and a washed-white shirt, helping him change into them. His clothes were now all a size too big for him, but very clean, cuff creases neat and tidy.

I arranged his tiger whiskers one by one, hanging down at both sides of the lips, then took out that old scarf he couldn't bear to throw away from the drawer, wrapping it around his neck. Covering the collar's strangulation mark.

"Be proper," I said, "going home needs to be proper."

Then I sat before the desk, writing a note:

"Suisui:

I am your father's old comrade. Your father has passed. Died of illness. He passed very peacefully."

I crossed out "peacefully," thought for a long time, still didn't rewrite it.

I folded the note, placing it on the nightstand. Only after finishing all this did I remember Suisui was still sleeping next door. I walked to his room door, hand on the handle for quite a while, finally didn't turn it. Let him sleep a while longer first.

I walked to the entrance. Suddenly saw a paper bag on the shoe cabinet by the door, medicine inside. Styptic powder, bandages, Yan Shen's handwriting—"Suisui, two packs each time, don't save."

I recognized that was bought at the western district pharmacy several weeks ago. That night, he went out alone to buy it. He probably waited for a very long time, until that day the pharmacy had stock. Then brought the medicine to the doorway, hadn't had time to put it in before never being able to return.

I stood at the entrance, holding that paper bag in my hand, not moving for a very long time. Finally I placed it beside the note.

Yan Shen died of illness. This is what he left before leaving. This is all the truth I know.

---

Lao Zhou helped me arrange his funeral. In the public memorial hall, only I came. Lao Zhou had to deal with Beastshield and couldn't get away; others had long stopped contacting. I sat alone on the long wooden bench, looking at that cheap coffin, two sentences going back and forth in my mind.

"Still be brothers in the next life."

"Don't let him know."

Later Lao Zhou arranged Beastshield work for Suisui. Starting from the bottom. Suisui didn't know the reason behind this, only thought it was a routine arrangement. He came to report in a brand new uniform; those eyes were identical to Yan Shen's.

I didn't contact him again, because I didn't know what to say if he asked.

When leaving the memorial hall, I ran into that child in the corridor. He was clutching that note in his hand, saying to me, "Uncle Zhao, when my dad left, did it really not hurt?"

I said, "He passed very peacefully."

Then I said goodbye, turned and walked into the snow outside. The sky was completely dark; White Capital's winter night was heavy and cold, snow falling on my overcoat no longer melting.

I have lived half my life, spent over ten years at Beastshield, fought in wars, carried guns, seen people die on the battlefield, and seen people die in the torture room. But the heaviest decision I made in this lifetime wasn't firing any single shot.

It was sending him off.

I won't say this was a correct decision. I don't know if it was correct. But Yan Shen entrusted his life to me; I couldn't let him down.

Every year when it snows, I think of him. Of him slightly bending over when changing into that set of unwearable uniform before the faint fire. Of him turning his head to say thank you when I handed him that cigarette.

Still be brothers in the next life.

I press the first cigarette I smoke on the night of the first snowfall each year into the ashtray, sparks on the cigarette butt bouncing once, extinguishing into darkness. I hand the second to the air beside me.

"Here, brother."

No one answers me. But I always feel like he took it.

Outside the window, snow is still falling. On that street he will never walk again, snow piles up, then is scattered by wind; White Capital's winter night is long and quiet. I still sit by the window, waiting for dawn.
