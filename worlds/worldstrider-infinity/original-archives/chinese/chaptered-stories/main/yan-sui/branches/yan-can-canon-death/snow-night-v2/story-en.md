# Snow Night

My name is Yi An. Bernese Mountain Dog beastkin, forty-six years old, former Beastshield blue uniform.

I killed my senior. He asked me to.

Every year when it snows, I think of that night. Think of him saying "let's still be brothers in the next life," think of him holding the cigarette and saying "thank you" to me, think of his hand slowly cooling in my palm. Think of all this, I sit before the window, watching snow fall, falling on that street he will never walk again.

So many years, I still don't know whether what I did was right or not.

---

The matter started from that winter night.

That night was unusually cold. White Capital's winters have never been warm, but those days were especially cold, wind blowing from the factory district, carrying the smell of coal smoke and rust. The fire in the stove had just gone out, I was considering whether to add coal, when I heard someone knocking at the door.

It was Lao Zhou. He was wrapped in an old overcoat, standing in the doorway, the expression on his face making my heart sink. Lao Zhou was my old superior when I was still at Beastshield, my promotion to blue uniform was signed by him. He rarely visited personally, especially in the middle of the night like this.

"Yi An," he said, "Yan Shen's in trouble."

My hands trembled while dressing. Not from cold, but that bad premonition drilling out from the marrow of my bones. Yan Shen was my senior at Beastshield, I went on missions with him for five or six years. Back then I was still young, hot-blooded, easily impulsive, caused him no end of trouble. It was he who polished me bit by bit—when I did wrong, he wouldn't scold me, just sat beside me, smoking cigarette after cigarette, waiting for me to figure it out myself. When people below me didn't accept me, it was he who took me to drink with other comrades, letting me speak out my grievances.

Half the credit for me wearing this blue uniform belongs to him. Others in the team didn't want to be officials, some found it troublesome, some had too wild a temperament to manage people. Those who clamored every day about wanting to climb up, their merits weren't enough and their character was hard to evaluate. Yan Shen privately consulted with us, saying he wanted to promote me. He said: "Little Bo isn't bad, just his temperament still needs polishing. But he's genuinely good to his own people. Let him rise, better than letting those with no filter on their mouths rise."

That matter was settled just like that. I stood at the side at the time, couldn't say a single word. Not that I didn't want to say, but something was stuck in my throat.

Later Lao Zhou told me how much effort Yan Shen spent helping me smooth things over for that promotion. He never told me. He never mentioned these things. He just patted my shoulder every time he saw me, saying: "Little Bo, do well."

Now Lao Zhou stood at my doorway, telling me Yan Shen was in trouble.

"Half a month ago he went out at night, went to find an old friend, after that no one has seen him again." Lao Zhou's voice was very low, "I asked around, someone said he was taken by a group of civilians, locked in a basement in the western district's back alley."

"Why didn't you tell me earlier?"

Lao Zhou was silent for a moment. "I also just found out."

I didn't ask again. White Capital this place, some questions don't have answers.

We crossed the western district's back alley. Snow had stopped falling, but the accumulated snow was still there, trampled solid, becoming a layer of gray-black ice shell. The alley was very narrow, both sides were old apartment buildings, wall paint peeling, light leaking from windows dim as candles about to go out. Lao Zhou stopped before a rusted iron door, pulling out a key from his pocket.

"This place was originally a warehouse," he said, "abandoned for many years."

When the iron door opened it made a harsh scream. Behind the door was a downward staircase, very steep, very dark, air permeated with an indescribable smell—mold, rust, and something else. I walked a few steps, that smell became stronger and stronger, until I realized what it was.

It was blood. And worse things.

At the end of the stairs was a wooden door. The door wasn't locked. I pushed it open, inside was very dark, only a dim yellow light bulb hanging from the ceiling, swaying slightly, casting shadows on the cement wall. The room was piled with some broken wooden crates and hemp sacks, on the ground was an iron pipe running from floor to ceiling, tied to it was an iron chain.

At the other end of the chain, was a collar. The collar was around a person's neck.

That person curled in the corner, back against the cement wall, knees hugged to his chest. He only wore a shirt torn beyond recognition, exposed arms and legs full of wounds—some were bruises, purplish-black, some were scabbed scratches, and some places skin had festered, oozing pale yellow pus. His tiger whiskers were gray-white, in a complete mess. The iron chain hung from the collar on his neck all the way to the iron pipe, length about only two meters.

I took a step closer. That person's body immediately tightened, head buried lower, hands protecting above his head, like a young beast trembling in a storm. His throat made a sound—not words, but a low, broken whimper.

I squatted down, wanting to see his face clearly.

"Yan Shen senior?"

He didn't respond. He just buried his head lower, body curling tighter. The chain was moved by his action, making a harsh friction sound.

I reached out, wanting to touch his shoulder. But before my hand touched him, his body suddenly bounced, like being electrocuted. His whole person shrank backward, until the chain held him back, unable to retreat further. A broken howl escaped from his throat—not anger, not fear, but a shattered, nearly animal-like begging for mercy.

"Don't... don't touch me..."

That was Yan Shen's voice. So hoarse it was almost unrecognizable, but it was indeed Yan Shen's voice.

My hand stopped in midair. I looked at this person before me—this person curled in the corner, collar around his neck, whole body trembling—how could I reconcile him with the Yan Shen senior in my memory. Yan Shen was that kind of person: when standing he was like a mountain, when speaking his voice wasn't loud but every word carried weight, when smiling his eyes narrowed into two slits, making you feel like even if the sky fell he could help you carry half. At Beastshield, I was contradicted by people, so angry I almost struck, it was he who pulled me to the back alley, handed me a cigarette, said: "Little Bo, it's normal for them to not accept you. I didn't accept people when I was young either."

That cigarette I still remember what it tasted like. Bitter and astringent, but the way he handed it was very steady, like saying—it's fine, I'm here.

Now, he curled at my feet, not even recognizing my voice.

"Senior." I called again, voice pressed very low, trying to make it sound not like a threat, "It's me. Yi An. Little Bo."

His body was still very tense, but those eyes buried in his arm slowly lifted. His pupils were turbid, like they hadn't focused on anything for a very long time. Then, bit by bit, his gaze focused on my face.

"...Li...ttle Bo?"

"It's me."

He stared at me for a very long time. So long I thought he was delirious again. Then his eye rims reddened. Not that kind of wailing red, but his eye rims slowly, slowly welled up with watery light, then that watery light filled up, overflowing from the eye corners, silently sliding down along the dirt and blood scabs on his face.

"You... how did you..."

"I came to find you." I said, "Senior, I'm here to take you back."

I reached out to undo the collar on his neck. That collar was leather, inner side had a circle of rough fuzzy edge, grinding out a deep purple strangulation mark on his neck. When my hand touched the collar's buckle, Yan Shen suddenly grabbed my wrist.

His hand was very cold. Fingers thin like withered branches, but the force so strong it startled me.

"Don't." he said.

"What?"

"Don't... take me back."

I thought he was talking nonsense. After being tortured for so long, his brain wasn't clear. I broke free from his hand, continuing to undo the collar. The buckle was rusted dead, I pried with my fingers for a while before prying it open. The instant the collar loosened, Yan Shen deeply sucked in a breath, like this was the first time in his life he was breathing.

"Can you stand up?" I asked.

He didn't answer. He just buried his head in his knees, shoulders slightly trembling.

I lifted him up from the ground. His body was very light, lighter than I imagined. A tiger beastkin, half a head taller than me, but his weight was like a bundle of dry firewood. His left leg—that leg disabled many years ago at Beastshield—now became even stiffer, joint swollen like a fist, completely unable to use strength. His arm draped over my shoulder, fingers unconsciously grabbing my clothes.

I half-carried, half-dragged him out of the basement.

Outside air was very cold, but very fresh. Yan Shen stopped the moment he walked out the iron door. He raised his head, looking at the sky. The sky was gray-black, couldn't see a single star, only sparks coming from distant factory chimneys. But he looked for a very long time, so long I started worrying if he was going to fall.

"Little Bo." he suddenly said.

"Mm."

"...It's nothing."

He lowered his head, didn't speak again.

I took him back to my place. My place was very small, but tidied up quite neatly. I put him on the bed, covered him with two quilts, then went to the kitchen to boil water. When the water boiled, I heard a very light sound from behind.

I turned around. Yan Shen stood in the kitchen doorway, one hand subconsciously supporting the wall, body slightly swaying. His left leg dragged on the ground, the joint at the knee bent at an abnormal angle.

"Senior, don't move." I said, "You go lie down."

He didn't answer. He just stood there, using a very strange gaze to look at me. That gaze reminded me of many years ago—back then I had just been promoted to blue uniform, people below didn't accept me, I threw a huge tantrum in front of Yan Shen. I said I didn't want to do it anymore, I said that blue uniform felt like borrowed clothes. Yan Shen listened, didn't persuade me, just said: "Little Bo, your temperament, you can't polish it by yourself. But you're genuinely good to us old folks, we all see it. You hang on a bit longer, if you can't take it come find me."

Now, he stood in my kitchen doorway, using that gaze to look at me. Not anger, not despair, but something more calm, something that made me afraid.

"Little Bo." he said.

"Mm."

"Help me with something."

"You say."

He looked into my eyes. His gaze was very calm. That calmness made my heart tighten.

"Kill me."

---

I thought I heard wrong.

"Senior, what did you say?"

"Kill me." He repeated. Voice not loud, every word bitten very clearly.

The kettle in my hand shook, hot water splashing from the spout, scalding the back of my hand. I put down the kettle, turned around, using a calmness I didn't expect myself to have to say: "You're crazy."

"I'm not crazy. I'm very clear."

"Clear my ass." My voice started to tremble. Not sadness, but a kind of anger I myself didn't quite understand—not anger at Yan Shen, but anger at this world that turned him into this, anger at those bastards, anger at myself. I finally found him, got him out from those bastards' hands, and now he tells me this. "Senior, I finally found you—"

"Little Bo." He interrupted me.

His voice was very light, but had something very familiar in it. That was the tone he used when giving me advice before—not commanding, but telling you a fact you would eventually have to accept.

"You still have a son," I said, "Suisui is still at home waiting for you."

Those two words were like a basin of cold water, poured on his face.

His whole person stiffened, then slowly, slowly lowered his head, looking at his own hands. Those hands placed on his knees, fingers thin like withered branches, nails still had black mud and dried blood traces.

"Suisui." he said, voice very light, like talking to himself.

"Right. Suisui. He's alone at home now, doesn't even know if his dad is dead or alive. Senior, I don't care what you experienced, you have to live and go back. Your son is waiting for you."

He raised his head, looking at me. Tears gushed from his eye rims, silently, flowing down along the wrinkles and scars on his face. But he was still smiling. That was the ugliest smile I had ever seen in my life—not because it was ugly, but because what was inside it was too heavy.

"Little Bo," he said, "look at me."

I looked at him. I saw the shadow of the senior who pulled me out from impulsiveness many years ago, but he was already different. His body was still that body, but something inside had shattered. Like he once told me—"Little Bo, it's not scary for a person to be knocked down, what's scary is not being able to stand up." Back then I thought he was talking about legs. Now I know, no.

"What do you see?" he asked.

I didn't answer.

"You see a former Beastshield," he said, "a disabled old man, locked up for over ten days by a group of civilians like a dog. You see a person who, when hearing someone say 'kneel,' his knees bend before his own will. You see a person who eats things from the ground, is walked around on a chain, is tied in the corner trembling."

His voice was very calm, like telling someone else's story. This calmness reminded me of how he used to give me advice—also this tone, unhurried, breaking down the reasoning and explaining it to you. But back then what he talked about was how to conduct oneself, how to manage subordinates, how to keep calm when things don't go your way. Now, he sat on my bed, wearing my pajamas, using the same calm tone to tell me why he didn't want to live.

"Senior—"

"Let me finish." he said, "Do you think such a person can still go back to be a father?"

I opened my mouth, but couldn't speak.

"Suisui will know." His voice finally started to tremble, but he still continued, "He will see the collar marks on his dad's neck, will see his dad not daring to look him in the eye, will see his dad curling into a ball trembling when waking up in the middle of the night. He will know what his dad experienced. He will know his father—that father he thought was very impressive, very remarkable—was actually just an old dog tied in the corner."

"Enough." I said.

"I'm not wrong, right?"

I didn't answer. Because I knew he wasn't wrong. White Capital this place, suffered injuries are never truly covered. They become scars, become nightmares, become that stone pressing on your chest when you wake up in the middle of the night. You can't deceive yourself, and you can't deceive the person living under the same roof as you. Especially you can't deceive a child you raised yourself.

"Little Bo." His voice became very light, like begging me, or like telling me a fact he knew I would understand, "I can't let Suisui see me like this. I can't let him know what his father became. I'd rather let him think I'm an unlucky bastard who died outside, than let him see me crawling on the ground licking food. Do you understand?"

I didn't speak. But I understood. Every word he said I understood.

When I was young and hot-blooded, I always couldn't understand why some things Yan Shen wouldn't tell me. Later I slowly understood—he was this kind of person. He carried all heavy things himself, feeling it was shameful to let juniors worry. When he helped me he never said anything, he thought that was理所当然. Now he wanted to entrust his life to me, he also thought this was理所当然.

Because I was his person. Was the one he personally brought up.

We sat there for a very long time. The fire in the stove gradually grew smaller, firelight jumping on his face, illuminating every wrinkle and old wound on his face clearly. He no longer spoke, just quietly looked at the fire. I looked at him, suddenly remembering some night many years ago when I went on a mission with him. Mission ended, everyone sat around the campfire, someone said, what if one day we don't come back. Yan Shen said, then at least die with dignity. Back then we all laughed, thinking "dignity" was the lowest requirement. Later we experienced too many things at Beastshield, only then did we discover "dignity" these two words were the hardest when alive.

Before it was him teaching me these truths. Now he sat here, entrusting his last bit of dignity to me. Before when I didn't accept people, he told me how to keep calm. Now he asked me to help with this, also telling me—Little Bo, this time you have to keep calm yourself.

"Senior." I finally spoke, voice very light, but every word like being gouged out from my throat, "What do you want me to do."

Yan Shen raised his head, looking at me. His eyes were red, but very bright. That was the first time that night I saw that still-burning thing in his eyes.

"You know." he said, "We've both seen it in the torture room. The least painful one."

My ears—that pair of Bernese Mountain Dog's distinctive droopy ears—in that instant completely pressed against my head. My tail also drooped down, clamped between my legs, tail tip slightly trembling. I couldn't control it. Yan Shen looked at me, looking at those pressed-flat ears, looking at that clamped tail, a flash of heartache in his gaze.

"Little Bo," he said, voice very light, "if you can't do it, I don't blame you."

When he said this sentence, it was the same as many years ago—not forcing, not commanding, just telling you an option, then leaving the decision to yourself. Like before when he sat beside me, smoking cigarette after cigarette, waiting for me to figure it out myself.

But I'm not the me of back then. I'm already forty-six years old, seven years younger than him, wore the blue uniform for over ten years, also led quite a few people. But the way he looked at me was still the same as back then—like looking at that impulsive junior who needed polishing.

"Senior," I said, voice somewhat trembling, but every word bitten dead, "You taught me before, the most important thing in being a person is being able to carry it. I've carried it for so many years, all taught by you. You entrusting your life to me is you believing in me."

My ears slowly stood up. I released my tail from between my legs, letting it hang behind me, tail tip still a bit trembling, but no longer clamped.

"I'll send you. Not because you begged me. But because you shouldn't walk alone."

He looked at me, looked for a very long time. Then he nodded. That movement made something in my heart shatter. But I didn't let it shatter. Because I knew now was not the time to shatter. I had to use everything he taught me before—keep calm, steady hands, finish the matter he entrusted to me.

"Alright." I said.

---

I didn't act immediately.

I needed to do one thing first—help him get himself clean. Not because of dirt, but because he was Yan Shen, was the senior I respected most in my life, was the one who polished me from a reckless kid into a blue uniform. He deserved to leave cleanly, with dignity.

I went to the kitchen to boil a basin of hot water, wet the towel, wrung it dry. I had him take off his clothes and sit on the edge of the bed, using the towel to carefully wipe his back. His back was half as narrow as in my memory; shoulder blades like two knives, spine protruding section by section. Below the ribs were all bruises, purplish-black, some already turning yellow.

I wiped very slowly. From shoulders to back, to that disabled left leg. The knee was swollen like a fist, skin shiny, purplish-red, burning hot to the touch. When I wiped there, his fingers dug into the bedsheet.

"Senior, does it hurt."

"It's fine." he said.

This was the third "it's fine" he said after coming back. I had already learned to translate this word—not that it doesn't hurt, but that compared to before, nothing counts.

After wiping, I searched through the cabinet for that uniform. Beastshield golden age's old uniform, military-green, with beast claw and shield badges on the shoulder.

"This is what you gave me back then," I said, shaking open the uniform, smoothing the wrinkles on it, just like how he helped me tidy my military appearance on the training ground back then, "You said you couldn't wear it anymore, let me keep it as a memento. I've always kept it."

I held the uniform before him.

"Senior, you look most proper wearing this."

He lowered his head looking at that uniform, fingers slowly stroking the worn-white badge on the shoulder. His eye rims reddened, but didn't cry. I spread open the uniform, helped him put it on. He was too thin, the clothes were a whole size too big. But I still helped him fasten every button, from the collar all the way to the bottommost one—just like before when he helped me tidy my military appearance, but this time it was me helping him.

He leaned back against the bed, quilt pulled to his chest. I pulled out the last cigarette from my pocket, handed it to him.

"Senior, you smoke this cigarette."

He took it, held it in his mouth. I lit it for him. Firelight brightened in the darkness for a moment, his face illuminated orange-red. He deeply sucked in a breath, then slowly exhaled. Smoke drifted up, dispersing on the ceiling. He lightly smiled. That smile was very faint, but more like him than any expression I saw in the basement.

"Haven't smoked in a long time," he said, "afraid Suisui would smell it."

Then he looked at me, passed the remaining half cigarette to before me. "Little Bo, you take a drag too."

I took the cigarette. Hand trembling, cigarette almost dropped. I inhaled a breath, very harsh, but I didn't cough it out. I returned the cigarette to him, he took it, smoked the last bit of ember.

"Do it," he said, "it's almost dawn."

I reached my hand into the quilt.

I'm canine. My hands are bigger than his, paw pads thicker, palm texture rougher. But I tried to be very gentle. His body stiffened under my touch—that was an instinctive reaction—then he relaxed, completely entrusting himself to me. He closed his eyes, breathing very shallow.

Outside the window, the sky showed gray-white light at the edge. I looked at him. His brow smoothed out, the corner of his mouth carrying a bit of arc, not a smile, but an expression of finally relaxing after long-term tension. He probably never showed such an expression in those fourteen days.

My ears pressed flat. Tail clamped tight. But I didn't stop.

"Little Bo." His voice was already very light.

"Senior, I'm here."

"In the next life... still be brothers."

My tears dripped on the pillow.

"Mm."

Then his body suddenly tensed, arched up, a very low sigh squeezed from his throat. Then, slowly, slowly fell back onto the bed.

No longer moving.

I didn't immediately withdraw my hand. That hand of mine was still on his body, feeling that warmth—I know beastkin bodies after death still maintain temperature for a period of time, dogs know, Bernese Mountain Dogs also know. I lowered my head, forehead pressed against that old uniform on his chest. The uniform fabric was coarse, with camphor smell, and a bit of bloody smell on his body.

I gently took his hand out from the quilt, held it in my palm. My paw pads pressed against his paw pads, his already a bit cool. Back then at Beastshield when going on missions, every time a mission ended, he would pat my shoulder, saying "Little Bo, you've worked hard." His hands were big and warm, patting on the shoulder with weight.

Now his hand was in my palm, no longer patting anything.

The room was very quiet. The fire in the stove was still crackling. Outside the window, that gray-white at the edge was slowly spreading open.

---

I carried Yan Shen home.

He's a tiger, skeleton big, theoretically should be very heavy. But now he was light as a bundle of dry firewood. My back is wide—Bernese Mountain Dog's physique has never been bad, but carrying a tiger beastkin walking on snow, every step sank very deep. His left leg dragged on the ground; I took one step, that leg slid on the snow once. I kept lifting it up, but it always slid down. When he was alive, he had walked like this for over ten years; now he's gone, still the same posture.

White Capital's early morning, there was no one on the street yet. Distant factory chimneys started emitting smoke, gray-black. I carried him walking step by step.

Senior, I'm sending you home. Just like before every time a mission ended, you said "Little Bo, time to pack up." I never knew how to repay your kindness. Now I use the heaviest thing in my life, to repay all your goodness to me.

Arriving at Yan Shen's doorway, I pulled out the key from his pocket. Key inserted into the lock, turned, click. The door opened.

The room was very dark, curtains drawn. The air had a scent belonging to this home. I carried Yan Shen into the bedroom, gently placing him on the bed. His head sank into the pillow, making a very light sound.

I stood by the bedside, looking at his face. That expression was still there, the corner of his mouth slightly upturned. I helped him smooth his gray-white fur. Then from the wardrobe I pulled out an old-style military-green underwear and a washed-white shirt, helped him change into them. The shirt was a size too big, but the collar button could still be fastened. I helped him comb his tiger whiskers, one by one smoothing them, hanging down at both sides of the corners of his mouth. Then from the drawer I pulled out that old scarf, wrapped it around his neck, covering that strangulation mark.

"Senior," I said, voice very low, "the last thing you taught me, I did it."

After finishing all this, I sat before the desk. I needed to write something to leave for that child.

Letter paper spread on the table, I picked up the pen, but for a very long time couldn't write a single word. The room was very quiet, only the old heater in the corner making slight crackling sounds. Finally I lowered my head and wrote a few lines.

"Suisui. I am your father's junior. Your father has passed. Died of illness. He passed very peacefully."

I stopped. Pen tip hung above the paper.

I crossed out "peacefully." He didn't know what was right, and I wasn't certain either. This thick black horizontal bar just lay on the paper, like all the unwilling things he had crossed out in his life.

I folded the letter, placing it at the bedside. Pressed it down with that stack of handwritten paper. Then I saw the paper bag on the shoe cabinet—styptic powder, bandages. On the paper bag was his writing, saying "Suisui, two packs each time, don't save."

A few weeks ago he told me, Suisui recently kept having nosebleeds, he ran to several pharmacies before buying this. That night he put the medicine at the doorway, hadn't had time to enter the house, before being blocked in the alley by that group.

I stood in the doorway, placing that bag of medicine beside the letter and that stack of communication coupons. Right at that time, I heard a sound from the adjacent room. Very light, even, steady. Suisui was still sleeping.

I walked to his room door. Ears—that pair of Bernese Mountain Dog's droopy ears—pressed flat. I placed my hand on the doorknob, held it for a very long time, in the end still didn't turn it. Let him sleep a while longer. Even if just one more hour, in this hour, he still has a father.

I turned and left. Passing the entrance I forgot my half pack of cigarettes on the shoe cabinet. Suddenly didn't want to go back to get it. Leave it for Suisui. When I first stepped into that back alley I was still a young man who didn't touch cigarettes for life, now I have to rely on this thing to make it until dawn. Just like his father back then.

I closed the door, walked back into the snow.

---

The sky was completely bright. White Capital's morning was gray and hazy, people started moving on the street. I lowered my head, walking step by step back to my own place. Footprints on the snow were still there, crooked two rows.

Walking, my ears—that pair of Bernese Mountain Dog's distinctive, soft droopy ears—slowly pressed flat. I let them hang at both sides of my cheeks, didn't lift them up. The short fur on the ears was wet from snow, clumping into locks, very cold.

But I didn't stop. I continued walking, tail hanging behind me, tail tip slightly dragging on the snow. Walking past that back alley, walking past that rusted iron door. The iron door was still open, the stair entrance pitch black, like a grave without a tombstone.

Returning to my place, I pushed open the door. The room was very cold, fire in the stove long extinguished. I didn't go to add coal. I just walked to the bedroom, stood by the bed, looking at that depression on the bedsheet—that was where Yan Shen had lain. On the pillow was a gray-white tiger hair, very short, curly. I picked it up, pinched it between my fingers, looked for a very long time. Then put it in my pocket.

I sat down, sitting on the edge of the bed. Then bent down, forehead pressed against the bedsheet, just like before when he patted my shoulder and I lowered my head. That fluffy Bernese Mountain Dog tail hung down from behind, tail tip drooping on the floor, motionless.

The room was very quiet. I've lived forty-six years, was a Beastshield soldier for over ten years, finally became a blue uniform. But the heaviest thing I've done in my life wasn't wearing that uniform, was taking it off after sending a person away. He was my senior, I was his junior. He entrusted his life to me.

I don't know if he chose me because he believed in me, or because that night only I was there. But I don't regret it. Not because I'm certain I did right. But because Yan Shen entrusted his life to me, I couldn't let him down.

Snow started falling again. White Capital's winter night is long, snow falling on the street, falling on the back alley's iron door, falling on that porch light he forgot to turn off at his doorway.

I took out that tiger hair from my pocket again, placed it on the nightstand. Then pulled out two cigarettes from the pack. One held in my mouth, lit. The other I placed on the pillow beside.

"Senior, smoke."

No one answers me. But I always feel like he took it.
