# Before Dawn

My name is Yi An. Bernese Mountain Dog beastkin, forty-six years old, former Beastshield blue uniform.

Two years ago today, I sent off the person I owed most in this life. He was my senior, called Yan Shen. I sent him off with my own hands. He asked me to.

Every year when it snows, I sit before this window, lighting two cigarettes. One I smoke myself, one I place on the windowsill. Snow falls on the windowsill, soaking that cigarette butt, and I don't move it. All these years I've wanted to ask him one thing, but I know he won't answer. He never answered me any sentence about this matter, except that night.

"Little Bo, let's still be brothers in the next life."

I called him senior my whole life. He called me Little Bo my whole life. Like it was just yesterday.

---

That night was unusually cold. White Capital's winters are never warm, but those days the wind was especially hard, blowing from the factory district, carrying the smell of coal smoke and rust. My stove had just gone out, I was about to add coal, when I heard someone knocking at the door.

It was Lao Zhou. He stood in the doorway wrapped in an old overcoat, the wrinkles on his face looking especially deep under the corridor light.

"Yi An," he said, "Yan Shen's in trouble."

My hands trembled while dressing. Yan Shen senior had been missing for half a month. The last time I saw him, he said he was going to find an old friend, then no more news. I had searched several times, only heard some vague rumors—someone said he was dragged into an abandoned warehouse in the western district's back alley by a group of civilians. I asked one by one, knocked door to door, no one was willing to say more. Later Lao Zhou got involved, he still held a position at Beastshield, had more channels than me.

Now Lao Zhou stood at my doorway, holding a rusty key in his hand.

We went to the western district's back alley. That alley I wouldn't dare stay in even during daytime, narrow and dark, ground covered with years-old coal cinders and frozen snow mud. A rusted iron door embedded in the wall base, after pushing it open was a downward staircase. Very steep, very dark. I walked down, that smell growing heavier—not mold, not garbage, but blood and fear mixed together.

Pushing open the door at the end of the stairs, I saw the most unbearable sight I had ever seen in my life.

In the basement, only a dim yellow light bulb was swaying. In the center of the room was an iron pipe, running from floor to ceiling. Tied to the iron pipe was an iron chain. At the other end of the chain was a collar, around a tiger beastkin's neck.

Yan Shen senior curled in that corner, back against the cement wall, knees hugged to his chest. Naked upper body full of wounds. Some had scabbed, some were still oozing pus, gray-white tiger fur covered with blood scabs and black mud. The iron chain hung from the collar on his neck to the ground; the length was only enough for him to move within about two meters.

I walked in. He heard the footsteps, didn't raise his head. His body instinctively shrank toward the wall corner, hands slightly protecting his head, as if long accustomed to this posture. A very light whimper came from his throat—not a cry for help, but begging for mercy.

"Senior." I said.

He stiffened.

Then he slowly raised his head. Those eyes were turbid like two dry wells; after quite a while they focused on my face.

"...Little Bo?"

"It's me. Yi An. I'm here to get you."

He cried. Not wailing loudly, but a muffled, broken sound coming from his nasal cavity, then his body began to tremble violently, tears gushing from his eye rims, washing out rows of white skin traces on the dirt.

I squatted down, undoing that collar. It was stuck very tight; the leather surface had worn the skin of his neck, the strangulation mark deep purple. I pried for quite a while before prying the buckle open; the leather surface was stuck together with his flesh, and when I pulled it apart he gritted his teeth from the pain, but didn't cry out. The collar fell to the ground, emitting a dull sound.

"Can you stand up." I asked him.

He couldn't stand up. His left leg—I knew this leg—had been ruined years ago, now swollen like a section of blackened old tree trunk, completely unable to bear weight. I lifted him up. He was lighter than I thought. A tiger beastkin, half a head taller than me, light as a bundle of dry firewood.

I half-carried, half-dragged him out of that basement. Walking out the iron door he stopped, raised his head to look at the sky. The sky was gray-black, not a single star, only sparks coming from distant factory chimneys. He looked for a very long time.

"Little Bo." he said.

"Mm."

"...It's nothing."

I took him back to my place. Put him on the bed, covered him with two thick quilts. His body was still shaking under the quilt, that shaking had nothing to do with the stove fire. It was another kind of cold.

---

When I wiped his body, he didn't speak the whole time.

Towel wrung dry, starting from the face. A gash at the temple still oozing blood. Downward, that circle of strangulation marks on the neck with purple skin turned inside out, skin already worn rotten. Further down, his body was much thinner than in my memory. Ribs floated one by one, like indentations on a stone slab. Arms, chest, abdomen, all wounds. New wounds叠旧伤, bruises套淤青.

When the towel wiped his left leg I asked, does it hurt.

He said it's fine.

Later I learned what his "fine" meant. Not that it didn't hurt. That this bit of pain compared to before was nothing.

After I finished I sat by the bedside, wanting to find something to say. He spoke first.

"Little Bo."

"Mm."

"I don't want to talk yet."

"Alright. You sleep first, talk after you wake up."

He closed his eyes. I guarded him there the whole night. Fire dying and rekindling, rekindling and dying. He woke up in fright several times in between, trembling all over each time, only relaxing after seeing me. When dawn came, he woke up, leaned against the headboard, head lowered, looking at those hands placed on his knees. Fingers thin like withered branches, nails still had dirt and dried blood traces.

Then he said a sentence to me.

"Little Bo, help me with something."

"What thing."

"Kill me."

The water cup in my hand shook; water spilled out in a splash.

"...What did you say?"

He repeated it. Voice not loud, every word bitten very clearly.

I looked at him. There was no madness in his gaze, no drunkenness. He was very clear. Clear enough to make me afraid.

"You still have a son." I said, voice starting to tremble, "Suisui is still at home waiting for you."

The weight of those two words made him quiet for a very long time. Flames jumping in the stove, crackle crackle. Then he spoke.

"In my present state, I can't go back to see Suisui."

"You can go back after recovering—"

"Can't recover." He shook his head. The corner of his mouth moved, not a smile. "Wounds can heal, some things can't grow back."

He lowered his head, slowly saying.

"They tied me in the corner, forced me to lie on the ground eating. In the first two days I could still fight back. They beat my crippled leg, stepped on my knee grinding it into the ground, I heard my own bones cracking inside."

"Then I learned. Not that I'm cowardly. I just didn't want to hurt anymore."

"I learned to shrink when someone pushed the door, learned to kneel when hearing commands. I don't remember how many days I stayed there. Time in there isn't time, it's round after round of chain sounds, and watching that door open and close."

"Suisui will see," he said, voice finally starting to tremble, "he'll see what his dad became. He'll know the wounds on my body weren't accidents. He'll know his father was tied up, knelt down, crawled on the ground licking. How do you expect him to live?"

He turned his face to me. His eye rims were red, but no tears. That dry red was harder for me to bear than crying.

"Little Bo," he said, voice very light, like telling me a fact I would eventually have to accept, "I'd rather let him remember a bastard who died outside, than let him know his father was an old dog tied in the corner."

The room was very quiet. I heard the sound of coal blocks collapsing in the stove. Senior used the way he taught me, breaking this matter down and explaining it to me—just like before when he told me how to live like a person in this rotten place.

Before, after every mission, senior would light a cigarette, stuff it in my mouth, say nothing. Later when I became a blue uniform and the people below didn't accept me, I threw a huge tantrum at his place. I said I didn't want to do it anymore, that blue uniform felt like borrowed clothes. He listened, didn't persuade me. Just took me to the back alley, stuffed another cigarette in my mouth, said: "Little Bo, your temperament, you can't polish it by yourself. But you're genuinely good to us old folks. Hang on a bit longer, if you can't take it come find me."

I didn't go find him. Because I later made it through.

But now he came to find me.

He didn't want me to carry something for him. He wanted me to make a decision he couldn't make himself. He had protected me so many times before, carrying all the heavy things on himself. Now he asked me to finish the last thing. Then he could rest.

I stood before the window for a very long time. Dawn was near; gray-white morning light leaked in. I knew once the sky was completely bright, I wouldn't be able to bring myself to do it.

I turned around.

"Alright. I'll send you."

---

I didn't act immediately.

I boiled a basin of hot water, had him sit up, and wiped his body one last time. This time it wasn't treating wounds; it was sending him off. He was even quieter than before, eyes closed, letting me lift his arm, letting me turn him over. His quietness made me very uncomfortable, because that wasn't the quietness of peace; it was the quietness of a person who had already accepted the ending.

After wiping, I searched through the cabinet for that uniform. He had given it to me, Beastshield golden age's old uniform, military-green, with beast claw and shield badges on the shoulder. Years ago he said he couldn't wear it anymore, hadn't even unwrapped the packaging, left it at my place as a memento. When I shook it open I felt a faint camphor smell, suddenly remembering he said this was something he kept as a memento when he first joined Beastshield, later his leg went bad, people left and tea cooled, he never could bear to throw it away.

"Senior, you wear this." I said.

He lowered his head looking at that uniform, fingers slowly stroking the worn-white badge on the shoulder. His eye rims reddened again, but still didn't cry. He sucked in a breath, held it back.

I helped him put it on, buttoned the buttons. Combed his hair for him, combed his tiger whiskers, smoothing them one by one.

"You look most proper wearing this." I said.

There was still an unopened pack of cigarettes in the cabinet. I found it from the nightstand drawer, took one out and handed it to him.

"You smoke this cigarette, as a ritual."

He took it, held it in his mouth. I lit it for him. He took a drag, exhaled a thin wisp of white smoke, then looked up at me and lightly smiled. That smile was very faint, but more like him than any expression I saw in the basement—like that Yan Shen senior who stuffed cigarettes in my mouth after missions many years ago.

"Haven't smoked in a long time," he said, "afraid Suisui would smell it."

Then he passed the cigarette over, said Little Bo, you take a drag too.

I took it and inhaled, very harsh. Bernese Mountain Dog's lungs aren't that tough, but I didn't cough.

"Do it," he said, "it's almost dawn."

I reached my hand into the quilt.

I'm canine. Bernese Mountain Dog's hands are bigger than his, paw pads thicker, palm texture rougher. That subtle roughness clearly transmitted back to my own palm with every movement. Before I used these hands to open letters, carry guns, also unwrapped the packaging of that uniform he gave me, never thought one day I would use it to do this.

His body stiffened under my touch, then relaxed like releasing strength.

Outside the window, morning light squeezed in through the curtain crack, a thin white line cutting on the floor.

"Little Bo." His voice was very light, like from very far away.

"Senior, I'm here."

"In the next life... still be brothers."

My ears flattened. Tail drooped down, clamped between legs. I didn't let it rise. But I continued. I held his wrist in my palm, fingers lightly pressing his pulse. His pulse under my fingertips was like a flock of birds flying wildly, then slowly slowing down, paused.

His body suddenly tensed, arched up, a very low sigh squeezed from his throat. Then slowly fell back onto the bed.

No longer moving.

I didn't immediately withdraw my hand. I lowered my head, forehead pressed against that old uniform on his chest. There were now some new things on it, warm, fishy-smelling. The uniform fabric was coarse, with camphor smell, and the bloody smell on his body.

The room was very quiet. The sky was already bright. The fire in the stove went out, only the first steam whistle from outside the window factory drifted in.

My tears only came down then. Dripping on his chest, soaking into that military-green fabric, becoming a small dark spot.

---

That morning I carried Yan Shen home.

He's a tiger, skeleton much bigger than mine. But now he was light as a bundle of dry firewood. Bernese Mountain Dog's back is wide, but carrying him walking on snow, every step sank very deep. His left leg dragged on the ground; I took one step, that leg slid on the snow once. I kept lifting it up, but it always slid down. When he was alive, he had walked like this for over ten years; dead, it was still the same posture, like he hadn't left this body yet.

Snow had accumulated all night at his doorway; when I stepped on it it creaked. The way he lay down there was heavier than on the way back.

I wiped him a second time; this time, it was for dignity. The towel inch by inch slid over his skin that had already lost temperature, wiping clean the last of blood and medicine smell. Then I searched through his wardrobe and found an old-style military-green underwear and a washed-white shirt, helping him change into them. His clothes were now all a size too big for him, but very clean, cuff creases neat and tidy. I arranged his tiger whiskers one by one, hanging down at both sides of the lips, then took out that old scarf he couldn't bear to throw away from the drawer, wrapping it around his neck. Covering the collar's strangulation mark.

"Senior," I said softly, "going home needs to be proper."

I sat before the desk, writing a note:

"Suisui. I am your father's junior. Your father has passed. Died of illness. He passed very——"

The pen stopped. Ink soaked out a black dot.

I crossed out the last two words. A thick black line. I didn't know what to fill in. I knew he himself hadn't figured out how to tell Suisui either, so he let me leave these three words. He passed very——the word after was crossed out by me, but it was still there.

I folded the note, placing it on the nightstand. Only after finishing all this did I remember Suisui was still sleeping next door. I walked to his room door, hand on the handle for quite a while, finally didn't turn it. Let him sleep a while longer first.

I walked to the entrance. Suddenly saw a paper bag on the shoe cabinet by the door, medicine inside. Styptic powder, bandages, Yan Shen's handwriting—"Suisui, two packs each time, don't save."

I recognized that was bought at the western district pharmacy several weeks ago. That night, he went out alone to buy it. He probably waited for a very long time, until that day the pharmacy had stock. Then brought the medicine to the doorway, hadn't had time to put it in before never being able to return.

I stood at the entrance, holding that paper bag in my hand, not moving for a very long time. Finally I placed it beside the note.

Yan Shen died of illness. This is what he left before leaving. This is all the truth I know.

---

In the afternoon I went to find Lao Zhou.

He was still holding a position at Beastshield, his office very cold, desk piled with a stack of yellowed files. I briefly told him about Yan Shen. After Lao Zhou finished listening, he was silent for a very long time. He stood up, paced a circle before the window, back toward me, the curve of his shoulder blades hunched down.

"What about Suisui." he finally spoke.

"Still at home. He doesn't know yet."

Lao Zhou was silent again for a while. Coal blocks collapsed in the stove, sparks splashed.

"Give him a position," he said, "starting from the bottom. Tell him there's just a vacancy."

"Alright."

Then Lao Zhou asked a sentence I would never forget in my life.

"Yi An," he said, "do you think what Yan Shen did was right."

I didn't answer him. I wanted to say he did it so Suisui wouldn't see him like that. So he could stand straight in his son's heart. But I couldn't say it. Because I'm not certain whether one person making decisions for another is always right. Even if this decision is sacrificing oneself.

But I could understand what he was thinking when he did it. Suisui was twenty-five that year, just entered Beastshield, still didn't understand anything. When that kid was surrounded and beaten, it was Yan Shen who dragged him out of the alley with his cane. Yan Shen personally told me that when he found Suisui in the back alley, Suisui was curled on the ground, tail broken, mouth full of blood. He said that was the first time in his life he couldn't even hold his cane steady.

A father's deepest fear isn't that he will die. It's feeling he's become his son's stain.

Before it was Yan Shen who taught me how to live like a person in this rotten place, don't be impulsive, carry things through. Now he used his own life to teach me the last lesson.

I walked out of Lao Zhou's office, outside the snow had stopped, but the sky was still gray. White Capital's winter night is long, long enough to make people feel the sun is fake. I stood on the street for a while, letting the wind blow away the frost on my ears. Then I glanced in the direction of his home, never went back again.

---

Every year when it snows I think of him.

Think of him curled and trembling in the basement corner. Think of him handing me that uniform that winter, me unwrapping the packaging and holding it in my hands trembling for a long time before putting it on. Think of him changing into that oversized uniform before the faint fire, lowering his head to look at the worn badge on his chest, saying thank you to me. Think of him saying let's still be brothers in the next life. Think of him slowly losing breath in my palm.

I've always wanted to ask him one thing.

Not "did I do right." I'll carry that myself, don't need him to answer. I wanted to ask if when I was wiping his body, when I handed him the cigarette, before he closed his eyes—did he remember that winter when I went to drink with him. He told me Little Bo, your temperament still needs polishing, otherwise what will you do when I'm gone.

Now he's really gone. I don't know if I've polished it out or not.

But he entrusted his life to me. He believed in me. That's enough.

Outside the window snow is still falling. I pick up that cigarette placed on the windowsill, find a thin layer of frost on it, long unable to smoke. I stuff it back in the pack, take out a new one, hold it in my mouth. Then I hand the second one to the air beside me.

"Senior, smoke."

No one answers me.

But I always feel like he took it.
