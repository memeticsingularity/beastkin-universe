# Snow Night

I killed my brother.

He asked me to.

Every year when it snows, I think of that night. Of the feeling of my hand on his body, of his final smile, of him saying "still be brothers in the next life." When I think of all this, I sit by the window, watching snow fall, falling on that street he will never walk again.

After all these years, I still don't know if what I did was right.

---

It started on that winter night.

That was two years ago, but I remember every detail. That night was extraordinarily cold. White Capital's winters were never warm, but those days were especially cold; wind blew from the factory district's direction, carrying the smell of coal smoke and rust. I had already gone to bed; the fire in the stove had just gone out, the quilt hadn't warmed up, when I heard someone knocking at the door.

It was Lao Zhou. He was wrapped in an old overcoat, face carrying an expression I hadn't seen in a long time. I had seen that expression at Beastshield—before going out on missions, when the superior came to convey orders, it was this expression. Not fear, but a heaviness of knowing the words about to be spoken would change everything.

"Lao Zhao," he said, "Yan Shen is in trouble."

My hands trembled as I dressed. Not from cold, but from a bad premonition drilling out from the marrow of my bones. Yan Shen was my old comrade from Beastshield, from the golden age together. Later his leg went bad, he retired, and we saw less of each other. But you know the kind of person he was—as long as he had one breath left, he wouldn't let people worry. For Lao Zhou to come knocking at my door in the middle of the night, things must be serious.

Lao Zhou told me the general situation on the way. Yan Shen went out half a month ago on a night, going to the western district to find an old friend; after that, no one had seen him again. Lao Zhou asked around and only heard a vague rumor—someone said he was taken by a group of civilians, locked in some basement in the western district's back alley.

"Why didn't you tell me earlier?" I asked.

Lao Zhou was silent for a moment. "I also just found out."

I didn't ask again. In White Capital, some questions don't have answers.

We crossed through the western district's back alley. Snow had stopped falling, but the snow on the ground was still there, trampled solid, becoming a layer of gray-black ice shell. The alley was very narrow; on both sides were old apartment buildings with peeling wall paint, light leaking from windows as dim as candles about to go out. Lao Zhou stopped before a rusted iron door, taking out a key from his pocket.

"This place was originally a warehouse," he said, "abandoned for many years."

When the iron door opened, it emitted a harsh screech. Behind the door was a downward staircase, very steep, very dark; the air was permeated with an indescribable smell—mold, rust, and something else. I walked a few steps; that smell grew stronger and stronger, until I realized what it was.

It was blood. And something even more unspeakable.

At the end of the stairs was a wooden door. The door wasn't locked. I pushed it open; inside was very dark, only a dim yellow light bulb hanging from the ceiling, swaying slightly, casting shadows on the cement wall. The room was piled with some old wooden crates and sacks; on the ground was an iron pipe running from floor to ceiling, with an iron chain tied to it.

At the other end of the iron chain was a collar. The collar was around a person's neck.

That person was curled in a corner, back against the cement wall, knees hugged to his chest. He only wore a shirt torn beyond recognition; exposed arms and legs were full of wounds—some were bruises, purplish-black, some were scabbed scratches, and some places' skin had festered, oozing pale yellow pus. His hair and beard had grown very long, gray-white mixed, a mess. The iron chain ran from the collar on his neck all the way to the iron pipe; the length was probably only two meters.

I took a step closer. That person's body immediately tightened, head buried even lower, hands protecting his head, like a young beast shivering in a storm. A sound came from his throat—not words, but a low, broken whimper.

I squatted down, wanting to see his face clearly.

"Yan Shen?"

He didn't respond. He just buried his head even lower, body curling even tighter. The iron chain was stirred by his movement, emitting a harsh friction sound.

I reached out, wanting to touch his shoulder. But before my hand touched him, his body suddenly bounced, like being electrocuted. His whole body shrank backward, until the iron chain held him back, unable to retreat further. A wail overflowed from his throat—that was a sound I had never heard before. Not anger, not fear, but a broken, nearly animal-like begging for mercy.

"Don't... don't touch me..."

That was Yan Shen's voice. So hoarse it was almost unrecognizable, but it was indeed Yan Shen's voice.

My hand stopped in midair. I looked at the person before me—this person curled in the corner, collar around his neck, whole body trembling—how could I reconcile him with the Yan Shen in my memory? Yan Shen was that kind of person: when standing he was like a mountain, when speaking his voice wasn't loud but every word carried weight, when laughing his eyes narrowed into two slits, making you feel that even if the sky fell he could help you carry half. At Beastshield, once on a mission my leg was blown injured; he carried me for three li, cursing me for being too heavy as he walked, but his hand never let go.

Now, he curled before me, not even recognizing my voice.

"Yan Shen." I called again, voice pressed very low, trying to make it not sound like a threat, "It's me. Lao Zhao."

His body was still very tense, but those eyes buried in his arm slowly lifted. His pupils were turbid, like they hadn't focused on anything for a very long time. Then, bit by bit, his gaze focused on my face.

"Lao... Zhao?"

"It's me."

He stared at me for a very long time. So long that I wondered if he was delirious again. Then his eye rims reddened. Not the red of wailing loudly, but his eye rims slowly, slowly rising with a watery light, then that light filled up, overflowing from the corners of his eyes, sliding down along the dirt and blood scabs on his face, silently falling.

"You... how did you..."

"I came to find you." I said, "I'm taking you back."

I reached out to undo the collar on his neck. That collar was leather, with a circle of rough fuzzy edges on the inside, grinding out a deep purple strangulation mark on his neck. When my hand touched the collar's buckle, Yan Shen suddenly grabbed my wrist.

His hand was very cold. Fingers thin like withered branches, but the force so strong it startled me.

"Don't." he said.

"What?"

"Don't... take me back."

I thought he was speaking out of anger. After being tortured for so long, his brain not being clear was normal. I broke free of his hand, continuing to undo the collar. The buckle was rusted shut; I used my fingers to pry for a while before prying it open. The instant the collar loosened, Yan Shen took a deep breath, like it was the first time in his life he was breathing.

"Can you stand up?" I asked.

He didn't answer. He just buried his head in his knees, shoulders trembling slightly.

I helped him up from the ground. His body was very light, lighter than I imagined. A tiger beastkin, half a head taller than me, but his weight was like a bundle of dry firewood. His left leg—that leg disabled many years ago at Beastshield—was now even more stiff, the joint swollen like a fist, completely unable to bear weight. His arm draped over my shoulder, fingers unconsciously gripping my clothes, like a drowning person clutching driftwood.

I half-supported, half-dragged him out of the basement.

The outside air was very cold, but very fresh. The moment Yan Shen walked out of the iron door, he paused. He raised his head, looking at the sky. The sky was gray-black; not a single star could be seen, only sparks coming from distant factory chimneys. But he looked for a very long time, so long that I began to worry he was going to fall.

"Lao Zhao." he suddenly said.

"Mm."

"...Nothing."

He lowered his head, not speaking again.

I took him back to my place. My place was very small—a bed, a table, two chairs, an old Beastshield flag hanging on the wall. I placed Yan Shen on the bed, covered him with two quilts, then went to the kitchen to boil water. When the water boiled, I heard a very light sound from behind.

I turned around. Yan Shen was standing in the kitchen doorway, one hand subconsciously supporting the wall, body slightly swaying. His left leg dragged on the ground, the joint at his knee bent at an abnormal angle.

"Don't move." I said, "Go lie down."

He didn't answer. He just stood there, looking at me with a very strange gaze. That gaze reminded me of many years ago—after Beastshield's golden age ended, we were kicked out one by one, and Yan Shen's leg was also broken by Beastshield after that. For a long time after that, when I went to see him, he looked out the window with this gaze. Not anger, not despair, but something more peaceful, something frightening.

"Lao Zhao." he said.

"Mm."

"Help me with something."

"Speak."

He looked into my eyes. His gaze was very calm. That calmness made my heart tighten.

"Kill me."

---

I thought I heard wrong.

"What did you say?"

"Kill me." he repeated. Voice not loud, every word bitten very clearly.

The kettle in my hand shook; hot water splashed from the spout, scalding the back of my hand. I put down the kettle, turned around, using a calmness I didn't expect myself to have: "You're crazy."

"I'm not crazy. I'm very clear."

"Clear my ass." My voice began to tremble—not sadness, but an anger I myself didn't quite understand, "I finally found you, got you out from those beasts' hands, and now you tell me this?"

"Lao Zhao—"

"You still have a son!" I almost roared.

Those two words were like a basin of cold water, splashed on his face. His whole body stiffened, then slowly, slowly, he lowered his head, looking at his own hands. Those hands were placed on his knees, fingers thin like withered branches, nails still残留着 black mud and dried blood stains.

"Suisui." he said, voice very light, like talking to himself.

"Right. Suisui. Your son is home alone right now, not even knowing if his dad is dead or alive." I walked before him, squatted down, looking into his eyes, "Yan Shen, I don't care what you went through, you have to live and go back. Your son is waiting for you."

He raised his head, looking at me. Tears gushed from his eye rims, silently, flowing down along the wrinkles and scars on his face. But he was still smiling. That was the ugliest smile I had ever seen in my life—not because it was ugly, but because what it contained was too heavy.

"Lao Zhao," he said, "look at me."

I looked at him. I saw the shadow of that Yan Shen who fought life and death with me many years ago, but he was already different. His body was still that body, but something inside had shattered.

"What do you see?" he asked.

I didn't answer.

"You see a former Beastshield employee," he said, "a disabled old man, locked up for over ten days by a group of civilians like a dog. You see a person who, when hearing someone say 'kneel,' his knees will bend before his own will. You see a person who eats things from the ground, licks others' shoe soles, trembles in the corner while chained."

His voice was very calm, like telling someone else's story.

"Do you think such a person can still go back to be a father?"

I opened my mouth, but couldn't speak.

"Suisui will know." he said. His voice finally began to tremble, but he still continued, "He will see the collar marks on his dad's neck, will see his dad not daring to look him in the eye, will see his dad curled into a ball trembling when waking up in the middle of the night. He will know what his dad went through. He will know his father—that father he thought was so impressive, so amazing—was actually just an old dog chained in the corner."

"Enough." I said.

"I'm not wrong, right?"

I didn't answer. Because I knew he wasn't wrong. In White Capital, injuries suffered are never truly covered up. They become scars, become nightmares, become that stone pressing on your chest when you wake up in the middle of the night. You can't deceive yourself, nor can you deceive the person living under the same roof as you.

Especially you can't deceive a child you raised.

"Lao Zhao." he said. His voice became very light, like begging me, or like telling me a fact, "I can't let Suisui see me like this. I can't let him know what his father became. I'd rather let him think I'm an unlucky bastard who died out there than let him see me licking food from the ground. Do you understand?"

I didn't speak. But I understood. Every word he said, I understood.

We sat there for a very long time. The fire in the stove gradually grew smaller; firelight jumped on his face, illuminating every wrinkle and old wound on his face clearly. He no longer spoke, just quietly watched the fire, watched those jumping sparks. I looked at him, suddenly recalling many years ago when we were together at Beastshield. Once before a mission, everyone sat around the bonfire; someone said, what if one day we don't come back. Yan Shen said, then at least die with dignity.

At that time we all laughed. Young people, thinking "dying with dignity" was the lowest requirement. Later we experienced too many things at Beastshield, and only then discovered that "dignity" was the hardest thing while living.

Now he sat here, entrusting his last bit of dignity to me.

"What do you want me to do." I finally spoke. Voice very light, but every word like gouged out from my throat.

Yan Shen raised his head, looking at me. His eyes were red, but very bright. That was the first time that night I saw that still-burning thing in his eyes.

"You know." he said, "We've both seen it in the torture room. The least painful kind."

My hands began to tremble. I knew what he was talking about. Beastshield's torture room had a mechanism—injury transformed into sexual desire, full value led to ejaculation, then death. This was a punishment, but those who had seen it knew that among all execution methods, this was relatively the least painful. On the battlefield, sometimes severely wounded comrades would beg you to use this method to give them a quick end.

I had never thought that one day I would use it on Yan Shen.

"Can't you do it yourself?" I asked. Of course I knew the answer, but I still asked.

Yan Shen lowered his head, looking at his own hands. Those bony hands trembled slightly before him. Then he raised his head, looking at me, the corner of his mouth pulling a very faint smile—that was the first time that night I saw a smile on his face.

"Lao Zhao," he said, "I can't do it myself."

When he said these words, his voice was very light. But those four words—I can't do it myself—were heavier than most cries I had heard. After saying these words he fell silent, just looking at me, tears swirling in his eyes but not flowing down.

I was nailed to the spot by those four words.

I suddenly understood. In those fourteen days, he had countless opportunities to seek death. He could have hit a wall, could have starved himself, could have provoked those guards to beat him to death. But he didn't. Not because he didn't dare, but because like every old veteran, he had an instinct carved into his bones—as long as there was one breath left, he had to hold on. This instinct let him survive on the battlefield, and also let him survive those fourteen days. But those tortures wouldn't disappear; those things he was forced to do, forced reactions, would follow him his whole life. He could never again be that "Yan Sui's father."

He couldn't do it himself. So he begged me.

He was begging me to help him complete something he himself couldn't complete. This wasn't cruelty. This was his trust in me.

"...Yan Shen." My voice was very light.

"Mm."

"You're certain?"

He nodded. That movement made something in my heart shatter. But I didn't let it shatter. Because I knew now was not the time to shatter.

"Alright." I said.

---

I didn't act immediately.

I needed to do one thing. Before I helped him on his way, I had to help him clean himself up. Not because he was dirty—everything they did to him in that basement could be washed away—but because he was worth it.

This was the last thing I could do for him as a brother.

I went to the kitchen to boil a basin of hot water, wet the towel, wrung it dry, then sat by the bedside. I helped him wipe his face. The dirt and blood scabs on his face melted in the warm water, revealing the skin beneath. He was actually still very young, just over fifty, considered middle-aged in beastkin lifespan. But his face already had too many things that shouldn't be there.

I helped him wipe his body. His arms, his chest, his back. Every place I wiped revealed more wounds. Some were fresh, red blood scabs still there; some had already formed purplish-black scars; some were old wounds—I had seen those old wounds many years ago, left from Beastshield.

He didn't speak the whole time. Just quietly cooperated, letting me lift his arm, letting me turn him over. His quietness made me even more uncomfortable. Because that wasn't the quietness of peace, but the quietness of having already accepted the ending.

But one thing I needed to do was change his clothes. Not give him old ones, but find that set of Beastshield old uniform in the cabinet that had only been worn once. That was over a year ago when Yan Shen sent someone to give it to me, saying it was left from when he retired from Beastshield, already couldn't wear it, but couldn't bear to throw it away, so passed it on to me.

I searched through the wardrobe and only found it at the bottom. That set of military-green uniform was a bit older than back then; when taken out, you could still smell faint camphor. When I shook it open, those familiar textures made me recall the scene of him sweating on the Beastshield training ground back then.

I placed the uniform neatly at the foot of the bed, then helped him sit up, like when we were young in the barracks tidying each other's military appearance, helping him put it on. He was too thin now; this set of clothes was a size too big for him. But I still fastened the first button, then the second, until the last one.

"You still look most proper wearing this." I said.

He lowered his head, looking at the uniform on himself, fingers slowly stroking the worn textures on the shoulder badge. His eye rims reddened, but he held back from crying. He might have thought of himself many years ago, wearing this clothes on missions in White Capital's snow.

"Thank you, Lao Zhao." he said, "Originally worried that after leaving, I wouldn't even have a proper set of clothes... now it's fine."

After I finished everything, I sat by the bedside, looking at him. He leaned against my pillow, quilt pulled to his chest. Yan Shen wearing that uniform finally looked like a person again. Finally looked like he had returned.

"Lao Zhao."

"Mm."

"Suisui."

What he said wasn't a complete sentence, but I knew what he wanted to ask. He wanted to ask—if someone asked, if Suisui wanted to know how his father left—how should I answer?

"I'll say he died of illness, passed very peacefully."

Yan Shen nodded. His eye rims reddened again, but he still didn't cry. He even smiled; that smile was very slight, but more like him than any expression just now.

"Got a smoke." he said.

I fished out half a pack of crumpled cigarettes from my pocket, pulled one out and handed it to him.

He took the cigarette, held it in his mouth; I lit it for him. He took a deep drag; firelight flickered bright then dim under the dim firelight, then he slowly exhaled a mouthful of smoke. Smoke dispersed before the dim firelight, blurring his face.

"Haven't smoked in a long time," he said, "afraid Suisui would smell it."

I looked at the ash on the cigarette in my hand growing longer, not smoking. Yan Shen quietly looked at me across from him, cigarette in mouth, ember bright and dim in the darkness. He seemed to have become that Yan Shen again—the Yan Shen who didn't show exhaustion and pain on his face.

"You can start," he said, "finish early, it's almost dawn."

I glanced out the window. It was indeed almost dawn. Snowlight outside reflected on the glass, a gray-white patch; night was about to roll into the clouds. After dawn, I didn't know if I could still bring myself to do it.

"Come." he said softly, "I'm not afraid."

I reached my hand into the quilt.

I tried to be very gentle. I knew my technique was never good, a rough man all my life, yet now afraid my fingers were too coarse and would hurt him. He had no reaction the whole time, just closed his eyes, occasionally emitting a very light pant. His body slightly stiffened under my touch—that was an instinctive reaction; fourteen days of conditioning made his body full of vigilance toward any touch. But only for a moment. Then he relaxed, completely entrusting himself to me.

The sky outside the window gradually turned light gray. Dawn's light shone in, illuminating Yan Shen's face. His expression was very relaxed, brows no longer furrowed, the corner of his mouth carrying a very faint arc. That wasn't a smile, but it made me more uncomfortable than a smile. That was a complete, long-term tension after—relaxation. He probably never showed such an expression in those fourteen days. Only when knowing he was about to be released did he truly relax.

"In the next life," he suddenly said, voice already very light, "still be brothers."

My tears dripped on his pillow. Then his body suddenly tensed, arched up, his panting squeezed into a very low sigh. Then, slowly fell back onto the bed.

No longer moving.

I didn't immediately withdraw my hand. I just looked at him like that, watching him quietly fall asleep. The sky was already bright; the morning shift steam whistle on the street sounded its first note. My tears only came down then, dripping on the quilt before him.

I bent down, picking up the cigarette that had fallen from his fingertips from the ground, extinguishing it in the ashtray.

But my hands were trembling.

I had killed people. At Beastshield, on the battlefield, face to face, white blade in red blade out. My hands had never trembled. But that night, my hands kept trembling.

---

I carried Yan Shen home.

His body already had no temperature, but it wasn't cold. Beastkin bodies were always like this; even after the person was gone, the body could maintain warmth for a while. My shoulder supported his weight—he was so light, so light it made my chest feel tight. He should have been much heavier. He should still have been that Yan Shen who could carry me on his back for three li in the snow.

His left leg dragged on the ground. I took one step, his left leg dragged on the snow once. I kept lifting his leg up, but it always slid down. Just like that word he said—hobbling. He had walked like this for many years while alive, and now dead, he still walked in the same posture.

White Capital's early morning; there was no one on the street yet. Distant factory chimneys began to emit smoke; gray-black smoke columns looked especially heavy under the gray-white sky. I carried him step by step, passing those mottled apartment buildings, broken streetlamps, shops with closed roller shutters. I had walked this road many times, but never felt it so long.

Upstairs, I opened the door with the key from Yan Shen's pocket.

The room was very dark; curtains were drawn. The air carried a faint, belonging-to-this-home scent—old wood, medicinal ointment, and a little bit of cooking oil residue. I carried Yan Shen into the bedroom, gently placing him on the bed. His head sank into the pillow, making a very light sound. I pulled up the quilt, covering it to his chest. Then according to the photo in the files, I crossed his hands over his abdomen. His tiger tail hung outside the bed edge, tail tip slightly curled, like it was alive.

I stood there, looking at his face. His expression was very serene, the corner of his mouth still retaining a bit of arc. That arc reminded me of the first complete sentence he said after being rescued from the basement—"still be brothers in the next life."

He wore back his own clothes—that set of Beastshield old uniform we had fished out at the last moment. That was probably what he truly wanted.

I stood by his side for a very long time, then turned to the kitchen to get a basin of warm water. Towel soaked, wrung dry. I helped him wipe his body, hands, carefully rubbing the dirt in his finger gaps and every nail with the towel. His fingers were rough and bent, traces of half a lifetime of labor. After wiping, I helped him comb his tiger whiskers one by one, hanging down at both sides of the corners of his mouth, white-gray long whiskers extending from the face, like covered with a layer of frost in the faint light.

Then I searched through his wardrobe and found a set of clothes he should rarely wear—military-green old-style underwear and a washed-white shirt, helping him change into them. That shirt was a bit too big for him now, but the cuff creases were clean and neat. I knew he wanted his son to see him leave properly, even if his son discovered him when he was already stiff.

"Be proper." I said softly, "Going home needs to be proper."

After finishing all this, I walked to the desk and sat down. I needed to write something to leave for that child.

Letter paper spread on the table; I picked up the pen, but for a long time couldn't write a single word. The room was very quiet; only that old-style heater in the corner made slight crackling sounds. I remembered Yan Shen once mentioned to me—this heater was something he dismantled from an abandoned warehouse, used for decades, every winter worried it would stop. This year it was still burning.

I lowered my head and wrote a few lines.

"Suisui. I am your father's old comrade. Your father has passed, died of illness. He passed very peacefully, without suffering."

I stopped. The pen tip hung above the paper surface; ink soaked out a small black dot.

I crossed out the last four words.

"When he left," I rewrote, "he was dressed very properly."

I folded the letter, placing it on the nightstand. Pressing it down with that stack of handwritten paper. I didn't look at what was written on the paper. The handwriting on it was Yan Shen's; that wasn't something I should read. But the moment I put down the pen, I suddenly realized that after that child woke up, no one would again say to him "I'm going out," no one would again insert a key into the lock and turn it once, no one would again hobble back in the snow saying "Suisui, Dad's back."

The sky was already completely bright. Outside the window came the factory's morning shift steam whistle; White Capital began another day of operation. People began to move on the street; in the distance was the creaking sound of a cart rolling over snow.

Yan Sui was still sleeping next door. His breathing leaked from the door crack, steady and deep, knowing nothing, sensing nothing.

I stood at his door, standing for a very long time. I wanted to push open the door, tell him his father was gone. But my hand was on the doorknob, unable to turn it no matter what.

Later I decided not to go in.

Let him sleep a while longer. Even if just one more hour, in this hour, he still had a father.

I turned and walked toward the door. When walking to the entrance, I looked back once more at that tightly closed bedroom door. Behind the door was his twenty-something son, his flesh and blood, his Suisui.

I closed the door and walked out.

White Capital's morning was gray and hazy. There were still not many people on the street. I walked back alone, taking the same road as when I came. Footprints in the snow were still there, extending crookedly from Yan Shen's doorway.

I lowered my head, looking at my own hands. That hand, before dawn today, had just sent off a comrade. He begged me to do it, and I did it too. Thinking of this matter later, I won't regret it. But I will always remember, remember his side face on the pillow at the end like he was asleep, and his thank you when he put on the military-green uniform.

Walking back to the broken alley entrance below my own place, I only then discovered a trivial matter.

I had forgotten that half pack of cigarettes on the shoe cabinet at Yan Shen's doorway. I hesitated whether to go back and get it, but quickly gave up on this thought.

Fine. Leave it for Suisui to smoke. When I first stepped into that back alley, I was still a young man who wouldn't touch cigarettes in his whole life; now I already have to rely on this stuff to survive until dawn. Just like his father back then.

My tears suddenly came down.

I leaned against the wall, in the gray-white daylight, just standing alone for a very long time.

---

I have lived half my life, spent over ten years at Beastshield, fought in wars, carried guns, seen people die on the battlefield, and seen people die in the torture room. But the heaviest decision I made in this lifetime wasn't firing any single shot.

It was sending him off.

White Capital's winter night is unbearably long; those few hours before dawn seem like they will never end. I made a decision that night—a decision that still makes my fingertips tingle when I think of it now. But I won't regret it. Not because I'm certain I did right. But because I'm certain of one thing: Yan Shen entrusted his life to me; I couldn't let him down.

That afternoon, I went to the eastern district, found Lao Zhou who was still serving at Beastshield. I briefly told him Yan Shen's matter. After Lao Zhou heard, he was silent for a very long time. The whole office only had the heater's crackling and the two of us sinking into our respective memories' breathing.

"What about Suisui." Lao Zhou finally spoke.

"Still at home. He doesn't know yet."

Lao Zhou stood up, pacing a circle before the window. His back view looked much older than usual, hair gray-white, shoulder blade arc hunched down. He stood there silent for a very long time, then said: "I'll arrange it. Give him a position."

"Give him a position," Lao Zhou repeated, this time voice heavier, "starting from the bottom. Give him a place to stay, a rice bowl to survive. Just say when arranging him there happened to be a vacancy. Don't let him know why, nor say it was my favor."

I nodded.

Then Lao Zhou asked a sentence I still can't answer to this day.

"Lao Zhao," he said, "do you think what Yan Shen did was right?"

I didn't answer him. I wanted to say he did it for Suisui. To not let his son see him like that. To let his father stand straight in his son's heart. But I couldn't say it. Because I'm not certain whether one person making decisions for another is always right. Even if this decision is sacrificing oneself.

But I can imagine what he was thinking when he did this. How old was Suisui that year, twenty-five, just entered Beastshield, still didn't understand anything. And when that child was surrounded and beaten, it was Yan Shen who hobbled on his cane alone to fish him out from the alley. That night, he personally saw his son's life threatened in that alley.

Even if that threat was directed at himself, he wouldn't have been so desperate. But when the target was Suisui, he couldn't bear it.

A father's deepest fear isn't his own death. It's becoming his son's stain.

So I can't refute. I can only let that sentence rot in my stomach, rot for many years, rotting into a lifelong heart ailment.

---

Every year when it snows, I think of Yan Shen.

Of him throwing his cane into the snow and going to fight people desperately. Of him curled in the corner trembling in the basement. Of his thank you when he put on that uniform and fastened the first button. Of his side face on the pillow at the end like he was asleep.

Of him saying—still be brothers in the next life.

I'm old. Hair white, waist not good, knees also hurt. But every year when it snows, I sit alone by the window, looking at White Capital's gray snow curtain, smoking half a pack of cigarettes. Then I knock the first cigarette's ash into the ashtray, and hand the second to the air.

"Here," I say to the air, "brother."
