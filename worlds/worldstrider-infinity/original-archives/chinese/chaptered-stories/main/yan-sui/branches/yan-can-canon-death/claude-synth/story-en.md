# Before the Snow Stops

I placed Yan Shen on the bed, then went to the kitchen to boil water.

The stove had gone out. I squatted down, used an iron hook to rake open the coal ash, added two pieces of coal, struck a match. When the fire came up, I stared at that cluster of blue flames for a while. Behind me, the bedroom was completely silent. I didn't want to go in.

But the water boiled. I carried a basin of hot water, wrung a towel, and sat by the bedside.

Yan Shen lay on his side, back toward me. He was awake; I knew, because his ears were trembling. Tiger ears, gray-white fur, dirty and black under the light. I reached out to touch his shoulder, wanting him to turn over. Before my hand touched him, his body suddenly shrank, his whole person curling toward the wall, a breath escaping from his throat—not words, but the sound of a beast startled.

My hand stopped in midair.

"It's me." I said, "Lao Zhao."

He slowly turned around. After a very long time, his eyes focused on my face. His face was very thin, cheekbones protruding, the area below his eye sockets all black. Lips cracked, blood scabs formed. But those eyes were still Yan Shen's eyes, amber-colored, only something inside had gone out.

"...Lao Zhao." His voice was very hoarse, like sandpaper scraping wood.

"Mm. Don't move, I'll wipe you."

I went to undo his collar. His hand suddenly lifted, grabbing my wrist. Fingers very thin, but the force so strong it startled me. His nails had black mud in them, embedded very deep, not something that could accumulate in a day or two.

"...I'll do it myself." he said.

But he didn't move. He just held on and wouldn't let go, breathing very rapid, chest heaving violently. I waited for a very long time before his hand slowly loosened.

I undid his collar, then saw his neck.

That strangulation mark was deep purple; the skin had already worn rotten, oozing yellow fluid. The collar was leather, with a fuzzy edge on the inside; when I undid it, the leather and flesh stuck together, emitting a very light sound. Yan Shen's teeth were clenched very tight, but he didn't make a sound. The collar fell to the ground with a dull sound.

I had him turn over, wiping his back. His back was half as narrow as in my memory; shoulder blades like two knives, spine protruding section by section. Below the ribs were all bruises, purplish-black, some already turning yellow. On his left waist was a very long scratch, scabbed, but the edge of the scab had curled up, revealing the red flesh beneath.

When the towel wiped his left leg, he trembled.

That leg had been broken at Beastshield many years ago. Knee deformed, originally unable to bear weight. Now it was even worse—the knee was swollen like a fist, skin shiny, purplish-red, burning hot to the touch. I lightly touched it; his fingers dug into the bedsheet.

"It's fine." he said. Voice very light.

This was the third sentence he spoke after coming back. Later I learned what his "it's fine" meant—not that it didn't hurt, but that compared to before, nothing counted.

After wiping the front, I saw his lower abdomen. There were wounds there too, but different from the bruises elsewhere. I recognized what it was. I had seen it too many times in Beastshield's torture room. I didn't ask. I just wrung the towel dry and continued wiping.

His tiger tail hung at the bedside; the tail tip had a tuft of fur that had been burned, a small charred black patch. I didn't know when that happened.

"Where's Suisui." he suddenly said.

"Don't know. Lao Zhou will handle it. You sleep first."

He didn't speak again. I helped him cover with the quilt, carried the water basin out to pour water. The water was very turbid, red. I stood in the kitchen, listening to the breathing sounds from the bedroom. That breathing was very uneven, sometimes stopping for a very long time, then suddenly sucking in, like a drowning person just pulled ashore.

I extinguished the kitchen light, sitting in the chair in the living room. Outside the window was black; snow was still falling, rustling against the glass. I didn't turn on the light.

---

I guarded him the whole night.

In between, he startled awake three times. Each time he sat up without warning, eyes opened very wide, looking at some point in the darkness, chest heaving violently. Then he saw me, slowly, slowly lying back down, like a deflated skin.

The third time he startled awake, he was sweating all over. Gray-white fur completely wet, sticking to his body. I sat by the bedside, using the back of my hand to touch his forehead. Very hot. Might be a fever, might be something else. I had no medicine.

"Lao Zhao." he lay there, eyes looking at the ceiling.

"Mm."

"Got a smoke."

I fished out half a pack of crumpled cigarettes from my pocket, pulled one out for him, and lit one for myself. He took it, held it in his mouth; I lit it for him. Firelight brightened in the darkness for a moment; his face was illuminated orange-red. He took a deep drag, then slowly exhaled. Smoke drifted up, dispersing on the ceiling.

"Haven't smoked in a long time," he said, "afraid Suisui would smell it."

I didn't respond. We just sat like that in the darkness, smoking, listening to the slight crackling of coal blocks bursting in the stove. His breathing slowly steadied.

"Lao Zhao." He took the cigarette from his mouth, looking at that ember.

"Mm."

"Help me with something."

I didn't ask what. I don't know if I guessed it, or didn't want to guess. I just sat there, watching the ash on my cigarette grow longer and longer.

"You know." he said, "The one in the torture room. The least painful."

My hand trembled. Ash fell on my pants, burning a very small hole. I didn't pat it.

"You're crazy." I said. Voice very flat, not like cursing, like stating a fact.

"I'm very clear."

"Clear my ass." I finally extinguished the cigarette, wiping it on the chair armrest, "You still have a son."

Those two words made him quiet. In the room, only the fire in the stove was still making low sounds. His eyes looked at the ceiling; there was a crack on it, extending from the wall corner all the way to the lamp base.

"In my present state," he said, "I can't go back to see Suisui."

"Recover and then go back."

"Can't recover." His voice was still very flat, like telling someone else's story, "Wounds can heal, some things can't."

He turned his head, looking at me. His eyes were very bright, abnormally bright. Don't know if it was from fever, or something else.

"Lao Zhao." he said, "Look at me."

I looked at him. I looked at that body thin to nothing but bones, looked at the strangulation mark on his neck, looked at that leg swollen purple, looked at his fingers that subconsciously curled up when I spoke. I saw.

"Suisui will see," he said, "He will see me shrinking before someone calls my name. He will see that these on my body weren't accidents. He will know his dad was chained, knelt, crawled on the ground licking. How do you expect him to live?"

His voice finally began to tremble, but he didn't cry. He just looked at me, eye rims red, but tears didn't fall.

"I'd rather let him remember a bastard who died outside," he said, "than let him know his father was an old dog chained in the corner."

I didn't speak. Because I knew every word he said was true. In White Capital, scars can't be hidden. You can't deceive the person living under the same roof as you, especially a child you raised from young.

"Lao Zhao." His voice became very light, like begging me, or like telling me something I already knew, "Help me with this."

I stood up, walking to the window. Outside was still black; snow hadn't stopped. Distant factory chimneys emitted gray-black smoke, scattered by wind. The sky wasn't bright yet, but already not so black; it was a very deep gray-blue. I knew that once the sky was completely bright, I wouldn't be able to do it.

"You're certain." I wasn't asking him. I was asking myself.

"Certain."

I stood before the window for a very long time. So long that I thought the sky was already bright, but turning back to look out the window, it was still that deep gray. Snow was still falling. The fire in the stove was still crackling. Yan Shen lay on the bed, eyes closed, that cigarette still夹在指缝里, long extinguished.

I turned around.

"Alright." I said.

---

I didn't act immediately.

I needed to do one thing first. I reboiled a basin of water, had him sit up, and wiped his body one last time. This time it wasn't treating wounds; it was sending him off. He was even quieter than before, eyes closed, letting me lift his arm, letting me turn him over. His quietness made me very uncomfortable, because that wasn't the quietness of peace; it was the quietness of a person who had already accepted the ending.

After wiping, I searched through the cabinet for that uniform. Beastshield golden age's old uniform, military-green, with beast claw and shield badges on the shoulder. Yan Shen gave it to me over a year ago, saying he couldn't wear it anymore, letting me keep it as a memento. I searched for a very long time before finding it at the bottom; when taken out, it had a camphor smell.

I had him put it on. He was too thin; the clothes were a whole size too big, the badges on the shoulder worn white. But I still fastened every button for him, from the collar all the way to the bottommost one.

"You look most proper wearing this." I said.

He lowered his head to look, fingers stroking the shoulder badge. His eye rims reddened, but he didn't cry. He sucked in a breath, held it back.

Then I had him lie down, pulled the quilt to his chest. I fished out another cigarette from my pocket, handed it to him.

"Last one."

He took it, held it in his mouth. I lit it for him. He took a drag, exhaled a wisp of white smoke, then lightly smiled. That smile was very faint, more like him than any expression I saw this night.

"Do it," he said, "it's almost dawn."

I reached my hand into the quilt.

I tried to be very gentle. His skin was very hot, much hotter than my own hand. His breathing was very shallow, chest rising and falling very slowly. I placed my other hand on his wrist, counting his pulse. Very fast, but very forceful. That was a still-struggling pulse.

His body stiffened under my touch. That was an instinctive reaction; fourteen days of torture made his body full of vigilance toward any contact. But only for a moment. Then he relaxed. Because he knew this was my hand, not those people's hands.

"Lao Zhao." His voice was very light.

"Mm."

"Still be brothers in the next life."

"Mm."

I quickened the movement of my hand. The sky outside the window was changing, from deep gray to light gray; dawn's light leaked through the curtain crack, a very thin white line cutting on the floor. I looked at Yan Shen's face. His brow smoothed out, the corner of his mouth carrying a bit of arc, not a smile, but an expression of finally relaxing after long-term tension. He probably never showed such an expression in those fourteen days.

His pulse under my fingertips grew faster and faster, then suddenly became very chaotic, like a flock of startled birds. Then, it paused.

His body suddenly tensed, arched up, a very low sigh squeezed from his throat. Then, slowly, slowly fell back onto the bed.

No longer moving.

I didn't immediately withdraw my hand. That hand of mine was still on his wrist, feeling for movement beneath his skin. Nothing. Nothing at all. But his skin was still hot, very hot, so hot it made me feel like he would open his eyes at any moment, saying "Lao Zhao, I'm hungry."

Beastkin bodies are like this. After death, body temperature remains for a very long time. Cools slower than human corpses. This warmth is more cruel than ice-cold, because it makes you feel like the person is still there.

My other hand withdrew from the quilt. Very wet. I smelled it; that was the smell. Semen gauge full. Damage transferred over, full, person gone. He had seen this in Beastshield's torture room, and so had I. We knew how this mechanism worked. But I had never thought that one day I would use it on one of our own.

I sat by the bedside, looking at Yan Shen's face. His eyes were half-open, pupils dilated, but the corner of his mouth still retained that arc. He looked like he was having a very good dream. Perhaps dreaming of many years ago, when he carried me on his back to the back mountain, cursing me for being too heavy as he walked.

The sky was already bright. The morning shift steam whistle on the street sounded its first note. White Capital's day had begun.

My tears only came down then. Dripping on the back of his hand, absorbed by that layer of still-warm skin. I wiped my face with the back of my hand, took a deep breath, stood up.

There were still things to do.

---

I carried Yan Shen home.

His body was much lighter than when I carried him. A tiger beastkin, half a head taller than me, now light as a bundle of dry firewood. His left leg dragged on the ground; I took one step, that leg slid on the snow once. I kept lifting it up, but it always slid down. When he was alive, he had walked like this for over ten years; dead, it was still the same posture.

White Capital's early morning; there was no one on the street yet. Snow had grown smaller, but was still falling. Distant chimneys emitted gray-black smoke, looking very heavy under the gray-white sky. I walked step by step, passing those mottled apartment buildings, broken streetlamps, shops with closed roller shutters. I had walked this road many times, but never felt it so long.

Arriving at Yan Shen's doorway, I fished out the key from his pocket. Key inserted into the lock, turned, click. The door opened.

The room was very dark; curtains were drawn. The air carried a scent belonging to this home—old wood, medicinal ointment, a little bit of cooking oil residue. I carried Yan Shen into the bedroom, gently placing him on the bed. His head sank into the pillow, making a very light sound.

I stood by the bedside, looking at his face. That expression was still there, the corner of his mouth slightly upturned. I helped him tidy his hair, smoothing the gray-white fur. Then I began to change his clothes. That set of Beastshield uniform was too big, not suitable for lying here. I searched through his wardrobe and found an old-style military-green underwear and a washed-white shirt. That shirt's cuffs had neat creases; he should rarely wear it normally.

I changed him into it. The shirt was a size too big, but the collar button could still be fastened. Then I helped him comb his tiger whiskers, one by one smoothing them, hanging down at both sides of the corners of his mouth. White long whiskers gleamed gray under the light, like dyed with frost.

I took out that old scarf from the drawer, wrapping it around his neck. Covering that strangulation mark.

"Be proper." I said, voice very low, "Going home needs to be proper."

After finishing all this, I turned to the kitchen to get water. I wanted to wipe him one last time.

Halfway through wiping, I heard a sound from the entrance. Very light footsteps, passing from outside the door, but not stopping. A neighbor. I held my breath, listening to those footsteps fade away, then continued.

After wiping, I sat before the desk, writing a note.

"Suisui:

I am your father's old comrade. Your father has passed. Died of illness. He passed very——"

I stopped. Pen tip hung above the paper; ink soaked out a black dot.

I crossed out the last two words. A thick black line, the two words "peacefully" cut off at the waist. I thought for a while, didn't write anything else.

I folded the note, placing it on the nightstand. Then I saw that stack of handwritten paper. Communication coupons. Edges yellowed, worn fuzzy by time. The top one wrote: "With this coupon, Dad will definitely talk properly with Suisui no matter what."

I didn't touch it. I placed the note on top of the communication coupons.

Then I walked to the entrance. On the shoe cabinet was a paper bag, medicine inside. Styptic powder, bandages. On the paper bag was Yan Shen's handwriting: "Suisui, two packs each time, don't save."

I recognized this. Western district pharmacy's bag. Several weeks ago Yan Shen mentioned to me once, saying Suisui recently kept having nosebleeds, he ran to several pharmacies before buying this brand of styptic powder.

That night, he went out alone to buy it. Medicine delivered to the doorway, but he never could walk in again.

I stood at the entrance, holding that paper bag in my hand, standing for a very long time. Finally I placed it beside the note.

Yan Shen died of illness. This is what he left before leaving. This is all the truth I know.

Then I heard a sound. Coming from the adjacent room. Very light, carrying a bit of dazed breathing.

Yan Sui was still sleeping.

I walked to his doorway, hand on the doorknob. My hands were dirty; nails still had Yan Shen's bloodstains and coal cinders. I wiped them on my pants, then gripped the doorknob.

I stood for a very long time.

In the end I still didn't turn it.

Let him sleep a while longer. Even if just one more hour, in this hour, he still has a father.

I turned around, walking toward the door. When walking to the entrance, I looked back once more at that tightly closed bedroom door. Behind the door was his twenty-something son, his flesh and blood, his Suisui. I don't know what this child will see when he wakes up, what he will think, how badly he will cry. I don't know. I will never know.

I closed the door and walked out.

---

Snow was still falling.

I walked on the road I came from. Footprints in the snow were still there, two rows, extending from Yan Shen's doorway, crooked. I lowered my head, walking step by step.

White Capital's morning was gray and hazy. People began to move on the street; in the distance was the creaking sound of a cart rolling over snow. The factory's work steam whistle sounded its second note.

I returned to my own place. Pushed open the door; the room was very cold, fire in the stove long extinguished. I walked to the kitchen, added coal blocks, struck a match. When the fire came up, I didn't look at it. I walked to the bedroom, standing by the bed.

There was a depression on the bedsheet, where Yan Shen had lain. On the pillow was a short, curly gray-white tiger hair. I picked it up, pinched it between my fingers, looked for a very long time. Then placed it on the nightstand.

I sat down, sitting on the edge of the bed. The mattress was still warm, where he had lain.

I fished out the cigarette case from my pocket; there was still one last inside. I held it in my mouth, lit it. Took a drag, then watched that cigarette slowly burn between my fingers.

Outside the window, snow was still falling. On that street he will never walk again, snow piles up, then is scattered by wind. White Capital's winter night is long, but the sky will always brighten.

I extinguished the cigarette in an old iron box by the bedside. The ash was white.

Then I sat there, in the already bright daylight, sitting alone.

---

Same moment.

Yan Sui turned over. His tail extended from under the quilt, resting on the bedside, orange-yellow, tail tip trembling slightly. He furrowed his brow, like having a not-so-good dream, but the corner of his mouth relaxed again.

Then he woke up.

The room was very quiet. Too quiet. That quietness wasn't the quietness of being empty, but a frozen quietness, like time had stopped flowing here.

He sat up, calling out: "Dad?"

No one answered.

He got out of bed, pushed open the bedroom door. There was no one in the living room; no light in the kitchen. He walked toward his father's bedroom; the door was closed. He pushed open the door—

Yan Shen lay on the bed, covered with the quilt, hands crossed over his abdomen. His eyes were closed, expression very peaceful, even serene. Gray-white fur was combed neatly; white tiger whiskers hung at the corners of his mouth. He wore that old shirt he rarely wore; the collar button was fastened to the topmost one.

Yan Sui stood in the doorway, looking at his father. His breathing was steady; chest didn't rise and fall.

"Dad?" He called again. Voice very light, like afraid of startling something.

He walked over, sitting on the edge of the bed. He reached out, touching Yan Shen's hand. That hand was still warm, the same temperature as in his memory.

"Dad," he said, "I'm up."

That hand moved. Or rather, Yan Sui felt it move. But it didn't. It just quietly rested there, fingers slightly bent, like holding something invisible.

Yan Sui lowered his head, forehead pressing against the back of Yan Shen's hand. Tiger whiskers rubbed against his cheek, warm and cozy. He didn't cry. He just maintained this posture, for a very, very long time.

On the nightstand was a folded paper, with a stack of yellowed communication coupons beneath it. Beside the paper was a pharmacy paper bag, containing styptic powder and bandages.

Yan Sui didn't raise his head to look at those things. He just held his father's hand, forehead pressed against the back of the hand, like many years ago when he was still a small tiger, lying on his father's back.

Outside the window, snow was still falling.
