# Before the Snow Stops — Final Draft

My name is Yi An. Bernese Mountain Dog beastkin, forty-six years old, Beastshield blue uniform.

Senior Yan Shen died on my bed. I sent him off.

---

That night I hadn't planned to go out. The stove was about to go out; I was adding coal to it. Outside the wind was strong, blowing the window frame creak-creak. I squatted in front of the stove, when I suddenly smelled a scent.

Not coal smoke. It was the smell of blood. And something heavier—fear. We Bernese Mountain Dogs' noses were trained like this since childhood. In Beastshield, tracking, alerting, searching, all relied on this nose. That scent was very faint, dispersed by wind, but I recognized it.

I put down the coal shovel, threw on my overcoat and went out.

That scent led me through two alleys. West District back alley had few people at this hour; the streetlamp had been broken for years, snow piled in corners forming gray-black ice shells. The scent grew heavier; I followed it to a rusty iron door of an abandoned warehouse. The door was rusted beyond recognition, with a crack at the bottom; the scent seeped out from that crack. I pushed hard; the door wasn't locked, making a screech when pushed open.

Behind the door was a staircase, going down.

I walked down.

---

In the basement only a dim yellow bulb swayed overhead. In the center of the room stood an iron pipe, running from floor to ceiling. Tied to the pipe was an iron chain. The other end of the chain was a collar, around a tiger beastkin's neck. That person curled in the corner, gray-white tiger fur caked with blood scabs and black mud. He was naked above the waist; one left leg dragged on the ground, knee swollen like a ball. The chain hung from the collar on his neck, length only enough for him to move within about two meters.

When I stepped in, he heard the footsteps. His body was faster than me—before I could speak, he had already shrunk further into the corner, hands protecting his head. That posture was very practiced, like trained hundreds or thousands of times. A very low whimper squeezed from his throat—not a cry for help, but begging for mercy.

"Senior." I said.

He froze.

After a very long time, the pair of eyes buried in the crook of his arms slowly lifted. Amber-colored; I'd seen them look down on people before, and seen them smilingly stuff cigarettes at me. Now they were ashen inside, like something burned out.

"...Little Bo?" Voice hoarse like sandpaper.

"It's me. Yi An. I'm here to get you."

He cried. Not wailing, but a muffled sound squeezed from his nose, then his whole body started trembling. Tears mixed with mud on his face flowing down. I squatted down to undo the collar on his neck. It was stuck very tight; the leather surface was stuck together with his flesh. When I pulled outward he gritted his teeth, making no sound. The moment the collar loosened, he took a deep breath, like breathing for the first time in his life.

I went to undo the rope on his wrists. Hemp rope had bitten into flesh; took quite a while to undo. His wrists were full of strangulation marks; where the rope was embedded the skin had already worn away, exposing the pink new flesh beneath.

"Can you stand up." I asked him.

He couldn't stand up. That left leg I knew; it had been ruined years ago, now completely useless, knee swollen and black. I hoisted him up, his arm over my shoulder. A tiger beastkin, half a head taller than me, but light as a bundle of dry firewood. I half-supported, half-dragged him out of that basement.

Walking out the iron door he stopped. Raised his head, looking at the sky. The sky was gray-black, not a single star, distant factory chimneys emitting sparks. He looked for a very long time.

"Little Bo." He said.

"Mm."

"...It's nothing."

I took him back to my place.

---

After putting him on the bed, I boiled a basin of hot water. He kept trembling; not the trembling of cold. I wrung the towel dry, sat by his bedside. He lay on his side, back to me. I reached out to touch his shoulder, wanting him to turn over. My hand hadn't touched him yet when his body suddenly shrank, curling toward the wall.

My hand stopped in midair.

"Senior," I said, "it's me. Yi An."

He slowly turned over. After a very long time, his eyes finally focused on my face. His face was very thin, cheekbones protruding, dark circles under his eyes all black. Lips cracked, blood scabbed. Those eyes were still Yan Shen's eyes, but something inside had gone out.

I went to undo his collar. His hand suddenly lifted, grabbing my wrist. Fingers thin like withered branches, but strength surprisingly great. Black mud in the nail crevices, embedded very deep.

"...I'll do it myself." He said.

But he didn't move. He only held me tight, breathing very fast, chest heaving violently. I waited for a very long time before his hand slowly loosened.

I undid his collar, then saw his neck. That circle of strangulation marks was deeply purple, skin already worn rotten, oozing yellow fluid. He gritted his teeth when the towel passed over. I had him turn over, wiped his back. His back was half as narrow as in my memory, shoulder blades like two knives, spine protruding section by section. Ribs below were full of bruises, purple-black, some already yellowing. A long scratch on the left waist, scabbed, but the scab edges curled up, exposing the red flesh beneath.

When the towel wiped to the left leg knee, I asked: "Does it hurt."

He said: "It's fine."

Later I learned what his "fine" meant. Not that it didn't hurt. That compared to before, nothing counted.

I had him lie prone, continued wiping. When the towel moved to his back, he suddenly spoke.

"...That fox beastkin," he said, voice very light, like talking to himself, "They said my son is Beastshield. I said I'm not anymore. They didn't believe."

I didn't speak. He buried his face in the pillow, voice muffled.

"...They kicked my leg. I couldn't stand up."

"Senior," I said, "don't talk anymore."

He paused. Then said another sentence, even lighter, like saying to himself. "...Suisui also got beaten that night. His medicine hasn't finished."

I recognized the injury on his lower abdomen. I'd seen it too many times in Beastshield's interrogation room. That wasn't ordinary bruising. I didn't ask, only wrung the towel dry, continued wiping. His tiger tail hung by the bedside, tip with a tuft of fur burnt black, a small charred patch. I didn't know when that happened.

After wiping I covered him with the quilt. He lay on his side, eyes closed, breathing slowly steadying down. I carried the water basin out to pour. The water was very turbid, reddish. I stood in the kitchen a while, listening to the breathing in the bedroom. That breathing was very uneven, sometimes stopping for a long time, then suddenly gasping, like a drowning person just pulled ashore.

I turned off the kitchen light, sat on the chair in the living room. Outside the window was dark, snow still falling. I didn't turn on the light.

---

He woke up in fright three times in between.

Each time sitting up without warning, eyes staring straight at some point in the darkness, panting like just pulled from water. Seeing me, slowly lying back, like a deflated skin bag.

The third time waking up he was sweating all over. Gray-white fur all wet, sticking to his body. I touched his forehead with my hand back. Very hot. Possibly fever, possibly something else. I had no medicine.

"Yi An." He lay there, eyes looking at the ceiling.

"Mm."

"Got cigarettes."

I fished out half a pack of crumpled cigarettes from my pocket, gave him one. He held it in his mouth; I lit it for him. Firelight brightened in the darkness for a moment, his face illuminated into orange-red. He took a deep drag, slowly exhaled. Smoke floated up, dispersing on the ceiling.

"Haven't smoked in a long time," he said, "afraid Suisui would smell it."

I didn't respond. We just sat in the darkness like this, smoking, listening to the slight crackling of coal blocks exploding in the stove. His breathing slowly steadied down.

"Yi An." He took the cigarette from his mouth, looking at that ember.

"Mm."

"Help me with something."

I didn't ask what. I just sat there, watching the ash in my hand grow longer.

"You know," he said, "the one in the interrogation room. The least painful."

My hand trembled. Ash fell on my pants, burning a very small hole. I didn't pat it.

"You're crazy." I said. Voice very flat, not like cursing, like stating a fact.

"I'm very clear-headed."

"Clear-headed my ass." I finally pressed the cigarette out, "Suisui is still at home waiting for you."

Those two words made him quiet. The fire in the stove was still softly crackling. He looked at the ceiling; there was a crack there, extending from the wall corner all the way to the lamp base.

"In my present state, I can't go back to see Suisui."

"Recover and then go back—"

"Can't recover." His voice was still very flat, like talking about someone else's matter, "Injuries can heal. Some things won't grow back."

He turned his head, looking at me. His eyes were very bright, bright abnormally. Don't know if it was fever, or something else.

"Yi An. Look at me."

I looked at him. I looked at that body thin to nothing but bones, at the strangulation marks on his neck, that swollen purple leg, his fingers that subconsciously curled up when I spoke. I saw.

"Suisui will see. He'll see me crouch down at the sound of people walking. He'll see these injuries on my body aren't accidents. He'll sooner or later know how they came. He'll know his father was chained, knelt, crawled on the ground licking."

His voice finally started trembling, but he didn't cry. He only looked at me, eye rims red.

"I'd rather he remember a bastard who died outside, than let him know his father was an old dog tied in the corner."

I didn't speak. Because I knew every word he said was true. In White Capital this place, scars can't be hidden. You can't deceive someone living under the same roof, especially a child you raised from young.

"Yi An." His voice became very light, like begging me, or like telling me something he already knew, "Help me with this."

I stood up, walked to the window. Outside was still dark, snow hadn't stopped. Distant factory chimneys emitted gray-black smoke, dispersed by wind. Sky wasn't bright yet, but already not that dark anymore, a very deep gray-blue. I knew once the sky was completely bright, I wouldn't be able to bring myself to do it.

"You're certain." I wasn't asking him. I was asking myself.

"Certain."

I stood before the window for a very long time. So long I thought the sky was already bright, but turning back to look outside, it was still that deep gray. Snow was still falling. Fire in the stove was still crackling. Yan Shen lay on the bed, eyes closed, that cigarette still pinched between his fingers, long extinguished.

I remembered many years ago. Back then I'd just been promoted to blue uniform, people below wouldn't accept me, I threw a huge tantrum in his house. I said I didn't want to do it anymore, that blue uniform felt like borrowed clothes. After listening he didn't persuade me, pulled me to the back alley, stuffed a cigarette in my mouth, saying: Little Bo, your temperament can't be polished by yourself. But you're genuinely good to us old folks. Hang on a bit longer, if you can't take it come find me.

I didn't go find him. I later made it through.

But he came to find me. Not asking me to carry something for him. Asking me to make a decision he couldn't make himself.

He was my senior. He polished me from a greenhorn into a blue uniform. He trusted me enough to put his life in my hands.

I turned around.

"Alright. I'll send you."

---

I didn't act immediately.

I boiled another basin of hot water. This time he didn't let me touch his shoulder, slowly turned over himself, sat on the edge of the bed. I wiped his body one last time. This time wasn't treating wounds; it was sending him off. He closed his eyes, breathing very light. I wiped from neck to shoulder, to back, to that ruined left leg. He didn't dodge, nor make a sound. His quietness made me very uncomfortable, because that wasn't peace; it was a person who had already accepted the ending.

After wiping I pulled out that uniform from the bottom of the cabinet. Beastshield Golden Age's old uniform, military-green, shoulder patches with beast claw and shield emblem. It was the year he retired that he gave it to me, saying he couldn't wear it anymore, let me keep it as a memento. When I shook it open a camphor smell dispersed; the emblem on the shoulder patch was worn white.

"Senior, you wear this." I said.

He lowered his head looking at that uniform, fingers slowly stroking the shoulder patch. Eye rims reddened again, but still didn't cry. He sucked in a breath, held it back. I helped him put it on. He was too thin; the clothes were a whole size too big. But I still buttoned every button for him, from collar all the way to the bottom one. Then helped him comb his hair, smoothed the gray-white tiger whiskers one by one, hanging at both sides of the mouth corners.

"You look most proper wearing this." I said.

He leaned back on the bed, quilt pulled to his chest. I fished out the last cigarette from the pack, handed it to him.

"You smoke this cigarette."

He took it, held it in his mouth. I lit it for him. Firelight brightened for a moment, his face illuminated into orange-red. He took a deep drag, slowly exhaled, then looked up at me and lightly smiled. That smile was very faint, more like him than any expression I'd seen since the basement.

"Haven't smoked in a long time," he said, "afraid Suisui would smell it."

He passed the cigarette over. "Little Bo, you take a drag too."

I took it, inhaled. Very harsh. Bernese Mountain Dog's lungs aren't that tough, but I didn't cough it out. I returned it to him.

"Do it," he said, "sky's about to brighten."

I reached my hand into the quilt.

My hand was bigger than his. Bernese Mountain Dog's palm is wide, paw pads thick, palm rough. His body stiffened a bit under my hand—that was instinctive reaction—then he relaxed. My other hand found his wrist, pressing his pulse. His heartbeat under my fingertips grew faster and faster, then became chaotic, like a flock of frightened birds. Then stopped.

His body suddenly went rigid, arched up, a very low sigh squeezed from his throat. Then slowly, slowly fell back onto the bed.

Not moving anymore.

I didn't immediately withdraw my hand. I lowered my head, forehead pressed against that old uniform on his chest. The uniform fabric was a bit coarse, camphor smell mixed with the bloody smell on his body. The room was very quiet. The fire in the stove had gone out at some point. Outside the window, White Capital's first steam whistle sounded.

My ears were flattened. Tail tucked between legs. Tears only came later.

---

That morning I carried Yan Shen home on my back.

He was taller than me, should have been heavier than me, but on my back the weight was like a bundle of dry firewood. Bernese Mountain Dog's back is wide, but carrying him walking in the snow, every step sank deep. His left leg dragged on the ground; I took one step, that leg slid on the snow once. I kept lifting his leg up, but it always slid down. When he was alive he'd walked like this for over ten years; now dead, it was still the same posture.

Snow had accumulated all night at his doorway. I opened the door with the key from his pocket. Inside was very dark, curtains drawn, air still holding a bit of medicinal cream and old wood smell. I laid him flat on the bed, his head sinking into the pillow, making a light sound.

I brought a basin of warm water from the kitchen, wiped his body a second time. That old uniform on his chest got a bit wrinkled from the wiping; I changed him into a washed-white shirt and military-green underwear. I found them from his wardrobe; the shirt was a size too big, but very clean, cuff creases neat. I smoothed his tiger whiskers anew one by one. Then pulled out that old scarf from the drawer, wrapping it round and round around his neck, covering that circle of strangulation marks.

"Senior," I said, voice very low, "going home needs to be proper."

After finishing these, I sat before the desk, wrote a note.

"Suisui. I am your father's junior, Yi An. Your father has passed. Died of illness. He passed very—"

The pen stopped. Pen tip suspended over the paper surface, ink soaking out a black dot.

I crossed out those last few words. A thick black line, placed after the word "very." I didn't write anything else.

I folded the note, placed it on the nightstand. That stack of handwritten communication vouchers pressed under the note. The top one wrote: "With this voucher, Dad will definitely talk properly with Suisui no matter what."

Then I saw that packet of medicine on the shoe cabinet. Styptic powder, bandages. On the paper bag was his writing: "Suisui, two packs each time, don't save." That was the thing he wanted to bring back the night he went out. He placed the medicine at the doorway, hadn't had time to enter the house, before never being able to walk in again.

I placed that packet of medicine beside the note.

Yan Sui was still sleeping in the next room. I walked to his room door, hand on the doorknob, held for a long time. My ears were flattened, tail drooping. I heard his even breathing, very light, very steady. He knew nothing.

I remembered when he was little, Yan Shen propped him on his shoulders to watch factory sparks. Suisui's small tail wrapped around Yan Shen's arm, warm and toasty. Senior told me, when Suisui was little and cried, he had to carry him on his shoulders and turn around the whole room.

Now Senior lay on his bed, wouldn't turn anymore. Next door that young man was still in dreams, didn't know his father had already walked the final stretch.

I didn't turn that doorknob.

Let him sleep a while longer. Even if just one more hour, in this hour he still has a father.

I turned around, walked toward the door. Reaching the entrance, I looked back again at that tightly closed bedroom door. Behind the door was his twenty-something son, his flesh and blood, his Suisui.

I closed the door, walked out.

---

Snow was still falling.

I walked on the path I came. Footprints were still on the snow, two rows,歪歪扭扭地 extending from Yan Shen's doorway. I lowered my head, walking step by step. White Capital's morning was gray; people started moving on the streets.

I returned to my place. Pushed open the door; the room was very cold, fire in the stove long gone out. I didn't add coal. I walked to the bedroom, stood by the bed. On the bedsheet was a depression, where Yan Shen had lain. On the pillow was a short, curled gray-white tiger hair. I picked it up, pinched between my fingers, looked for a long time.

I sat down, sat on the edge of the bed. The mattress was still warm, where he had lain.

I fished out the cigarette pack from my pocket. Empty. The last one had already been given to him. I placed the empty pack on the nightstand, beside that tiger hair. Then I bent down, forehead pressed against the bedsheet. Ears flattened, tail drooping on the floor, motionless.

Senior used to tell me, Little Bo, your temperament still needs polishing, otherwise what will you do when I'm gone.

Now he's really gone. I don't know if I've polished it out or not.

But he put his life in my hands. He trusted me. That's enough.

---

At the same moment.

Yan Sui turned over. Tail stretched out from under the quilt, draping by the bedside, orange-yellow, tip slightly trembling. He frowned, like having a not-so-good dream, but the corner of his mouth relaxed again.

Then he woke up.

The room was very quiet. Too quiet. That quiet wasn't the quiet of emptiness, but a frozen quiet, like time had stopped flowing here.

He sat up, called out: "Dad?"

No one answered.

He got off the bed, pushed open the bedroom door. No one in the living room. No light in the kitchen. He walked toward his father's bedroom; the door was closed. He pushed open the door—

Yan Shen lay on the bed, covered with quilt, hands folded over his abdomen. Eyes closed, expression very calm, could even be called peaceful. Gray-white fur was combed neatly, white tiger whiskers hanging at the mouth corners. He wore that rarely worn old shirt; the collar button was fastened to the top one.

Yan Sui stood in the doorway, looking at his father. Chest not rising and falling.

"Dad?" He called again. Voice very light, like afraid of startling something.

He walked over, sat on the edge of the bed. Reached out, touched Yan Shen's hand. That hand was still warm, the same temperature as in his memory.

"Dad," he said, "I'm up."

That hand moved. Or rather, Yan Sui felt it move. But it didn't. It only quietly lay there, fingers slightly curved, like holding something invisible.

Yan Sui lowered his head, forehead pressed against the back of Yan Shen's hand. Tiger whiskers brushed his cheek, warm and toasty. He didn't cry. He only maintained this posture, for a very, very long time.

On the nightstand was a folded paper, under it pressed a stack of yellowed communication vouchers. Beside the paper was a pharmacy paper bag, containing styptic powder and bandages. On the paper bag was father's handwriting.

Yan Sui didn't look up at those things. He only held his father's hand, forehead pressed against the back of his hand, like many years ago when he was still a small tiger, lying on his father's back.

Outside the window, snow was still falling.
